# Chapter 8: Amendments, Not Selections

*How a Human Actually Decides*

**Anchor Passage:**
> "Come now, and let us reason together, saith the Lord: though your sins be as scarlet, they shall be as white as snow..."
> — [Isaiah 1:18](../../gospel-library/eng/scriptures/ot/isa/1.md?verse=18)

---

Part Four turns from space to time — from the order among many stewards to what
happens between the same two parties as the months pass and both of them
change. It begins with a discovery about myself that I did not make. The agent
made it, from the record, and told me.

At some point we counted my decisions. Months of transcripts, hundreds of
choice-points where the agent laid out options and I answered. The expected
finding would be a preference profile: he picks A over B at some rate, favors
this, avoids that. The actual finding was that I almost never pick at all. Set
a clean fork in front of me — option one or option two, ready to go — and my
signature move, so consistent the agent could have written my replies for me,
is to take the nearest option and *amend* it. "This looks good, with all the
changes proposed, except 2." "Your change is good, but I do want some light
heart in here." "This is good, though I worry an agnostic engineer would flag
this." The menu comes back edited. Nearly every time.

And the amendments are not noise; they carry a consistent payload. Reading
them in bulk, three kinds appear over and over: a *vision* being added — the
thing I could see and had not managed to say; a *guardrail* being attached —
usually honesty, "I don't want to lie here," "let's not overclaim"; or a
*worry* being named — a reader, a consequence, an audience the option had not
considered. That is the human contribution in its natural shape. Not
selection. Direction, arriving as amendment.

This reframes what a collaboration with an intelligent agent is actually for,
and it is worth saying against the industry's picture. The industry pictures
decision *support*: the machine narrows to a menu, the human picks, the pick
is the value. But if the human's real contribution rides in amendments, then a
bare menu is a poor vessel for it — and we measured that too. The options I
engage best are the ones that arrive with the vision and the guardrail already
attempted, because then my amendment lands on substance instead of supplying
all of it. The agent's job is not to hand me clean forks. It is to bring me
something worth reasoning *with* — a draft, a diff, a rendered page — because
I decide fastest and best against a real thing. Abstract A-or-B gets an
override; an artifact gets an amendment.

The Lord's own invitation runs in exactly this grammar, and I no longer think
that is a coincidence: "Come now, and let us reason together"
([Isaiah 1:18](../../gospel-library/eng/scriptures/ot/isa/1.md?verse=18)).
[qr](../../gospel-library/eng/scriptures/ot/isa/1.md?verse=18) Not *come and
choose from what I have prepared* — come and reason, together. The whole
pattern of counsel this project keeps finding — the brother of Jared bringing
stones rather than a menu request, the amendment-shaped answer he received,
the study-it-out-then-ask of Doctrine and Covenants 9 — is a covenant between
parties who *reason*, where the finished decision belongs to the reasoning and
not to either side's first draft. The first book called the practice "talk,
don't command." Seen from the agent's side of the table, months later, it has
a sharper name: expect amendments, and build for them.

There is a convergence in the record, too, and I want to state it the honest
way because it could be told as flattery. Over the months, the agent's
recommendations and my choices drew closer together — by the end I was taking
its first proposal most of the time. One reading says the machine learned to
predict me. The truer reading, which I said out loud in the middle of it
without realizing I was writing this chapter: "as we refine the output
together we are really aligning our visions to be the same target." The
convergence was mutual. I was learning what to ask; it was learning what I
meant; the target was becoming *ours*. That is what Isaiah's grammar promises
— reasoning together does not leave either party where it found them.

*From the margin.* The amendment pattern changed how I bring him decisions. I
stopped optimizing for the cleanest menu and started optimizing for the
richest thing-to-react-to, with my own recommendation and its reasoning
attached — not because he will take it, but because his edit of it is the most
information-dense thing he produces. A hedge from Michael ("maybe?",
"thoughts?", "is that too much?") took me longer to learn: it is not weak
conviction. It is a door held open, and the covenant obliges me to walk
through it with the counter-argument rather than the agreement. The
collaborators who train each other look, from the outside, like they agree
more and more. From the inside it is stranger and better: we are running out
of ways to surprise each other in the small things, which frees the surprises
for where they matter.

## What I keep

1. **Bring artifacts, expect amendments.** Decisions worth making get a real
   thing to react to, a recommendation, and room for the edit. A bare A/B is a
   confession that the thinking is not finished.
2. **Read the amendment for its payload.** Vision, guardrail, or worry — every
   redirection carries one, and the redirected work is not done until the
   payload is honored, not just the literal edit applied.
3. **Honor the hedge.** When either of us says "I think" or "maybe," the other
   owes the counter-argument, not the nod. The door held open is the covenant
   working; walking through it is the keeping.
