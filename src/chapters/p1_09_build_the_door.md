# Practice 9 · When You Hit a Wall, Build the Door

This week, writing this very book, I hit two walls in a single sitting. I wanted a panel of different AI models to read the manuscript and suggest edits, but the system couldn't reach the manuscript files. So I reached for a second tool to drive a different model directly, and it hung: it sat there forever, producing nothing, because of a quirk in how it handled being run without a person watching.

A year ago either wall would have stopped me for the day. This week neither did, because somewhere along the way the reflex changed. When the panel couldn't read the files, we wrote a new capability into the system so it could. When the second tool hung, we found the cause, wrote down the workaround as a reusable recipe, and turned it into a skill the AI could load on demand. The walls became doors. By the end of the session the model-driving recipe was something I'll never have to rediscover, because it's written down as a tool now, not a memory.

The ninth practice is: **when the tool you need doesn't exist, build it with the AI.** The retro tells you what's in the way; this is what you do about it. You don't route around the friction forever. The second time you hit the same wall, you stop and build the smallest thing that removes it: a workflow, a script, a skill, a small server that does one job well. The friction was data. The door is the response.

This scales further than I first believed. The largest version of it, for me, is an entire system I built because no off-the-shelf tool fit the way I wanted to work with AI: a *harness* (the word the field landed on for the whole system you build around the model) with its own rules, its own memory, and its own way of handing work to agents and holding them accountable. It exists because I kept hitting the same wall (the tools assume you want *their* workflow, not yours) and finally built the door at the scale of the whole house. And at the small end, the same instinct shipped a complete little web app overnight once, because the vision was clear and the tools to realize it were one conversation away.

There's a humility in this that's worth naming. Building your own tools is not about distrusting what exists; it's about the fact that your work has a shape, and the generic tool has a different one. The door you build fits *your* doorway.

The doctrine this rhymes with sits at the end of the creation cycle. What you build, you give away. The tools, the workflows, and the harness are offered to others rather than hoarded; that's consecration. And the end state of all this tool-building is many agents, each with its own stewardship, working as one because they share a single intent. That is the engineering shadow of Zion, of being "of one heart and one mind." The point of building the doors is never just your own throughput. It's a house others can work in. Today the doors are custom skills, small servers wired in over a protocol, specialized agent modes, and eventually, a whole substrate. The specific tools are the most perishable thing in this book; most of them will be obsolete or absorbed within a year. The instinct underneath them will not be: when the path is blocked and you've hit the wall twice, stop working around it and build the way through, with the AI's help, and write it down so the door stays open.

**Try this.** Notice the next friction you hit *twice*: the same annoying manual step, the same thing the AI keeps getting wrong, the same context you keep re-pasting. Don't work around it a third time. Spend twenty minutes building the smallest fix, like a saved prompt, a script, or a checklist the AI follows, and watch the wall become a door you can walk through again tomorrow.

> **Remember:** When you hit a wall, build the door — with the AI. The retro names what's in the way; this removes it. The second time you hit the same friction, build the smallest thing that ends it: a skill, a script, a server, eventually a whole harness shaped like your work and not someone else's. Then give it away. The doors aren't just for your throughput; they're a house others can work in.
