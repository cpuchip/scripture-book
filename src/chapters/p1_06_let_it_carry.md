# Practice 6 · Let It Carry What You Can't

In late 2025 I built seven networked multiplayer games into a single app, in a programming language I have never written a line of. The language was Dart. I'd never used it. I'd also never wired up the kind of real-time networking the games needed. I had a vision of what the games should do and a plan to get there. I directed the work file by file, but I never touched the Dart code or the networking plumbing. The AI carried what I couldn't. The games ran, on two operating systems, and my kids played them.

Later I did it again at a different scale. The system that runs much of our automated study work is built in Rust and SQL, which are also foreign to me. I had the vision; I set the bounds; I verified the results. The thing got built in languages that are, to me, effectively foreign.

The sixth practice is: **let it carry what you can't, and verify the fruit.** Stewardship is how one person does more than one person can do, and that includes more than one person *knows* how to do. You are not limited to the work you could type yourself. You are limited to the work you can *envision clearly enough to direct, and judge well enough to trust.* Those are larger boundaries, and they're the ones worth pushing.

There is a discipline that keeps this from being reckless, and it's the same one from a few practices back: you verify. I don't read Rust fluently, but I can run the system, watch what it does, and tell you whether it's right. The verification doesn't require me to write the language; it requires me to know the *outcome* I asked for. Delegation without verification is abdication. Delegation with verification is reach.

The inverse failure taught me the other half. Early on I asked the AI to find scriptures on a topic, and it gave me a shallow list from a keyword search. The list was fine, but shallow. When we used a search that understands meaning instead of just words, it surfaced a passage I'd never have found otherwise. The lesson: letting it carry the work also means letting it use the tools that do the work well, not just the obvious ones.

This pattern is the oldest management advice in scripture. Moses tried to judge all of Israel by himself until his father-in-law told him the load was too heavy. The answer was to appoint capable men and hand them authority over portions of the work. When Christ sent the Twelve, He didn't send them to watch; He gave them power and a defined errand and let them go. Authority handed over, within bounds, is how the work scales past the one person at the center. (Part Two: Stewardship — Jethro and the Twelve; Physical Creation — the work that actually gets made.)

Today this looks like sub-agents doing research, semantic search that beats keyword, and a division of labor across several models. The specific tools will turn over. What won't: hand the AI the parts you can't do yourself or shouldn't do by hand, give it the tools to do them well, and judge the result by the outcome you envisioned.

**Try this.** Name one thing you've been doing by hand because you "have to": a language you half-know, a refactor you dread, or research you keep putting off. Hand it to the AI with a clear bound and a way for you to verify the result. You don't have to know how to do it. You have to know what *done* looks like.

> **Remember:** Let it carry what you can't. You're not limited to the work you could type yourself — only to the work you can envision clearly enough to direct and judge well enough to trust. Delegation without verification is abdication; delegation with verification is reach. Give it the right tools, then judge by the outcome.
