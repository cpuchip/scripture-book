# Coda · Go Touch Some Grass

The clearest idea I've had about this book did not come at the keyboard. It came when I was away from screens for a few hours, not thinking about it on purpose. The shape of the whole thing, which I'd been wrestling for days, simply arrived. Later I described the problem out loud to my daughter, with no computer between us, and she saw the answer before I'd finished explaining the question.

I've noticed the pattern enough times to trust it now, and it runs the other way too: the sessions where I ground at the same wall for hours, getting nowhere, were almost always the sessions where I hadn't stepped away. The breakthrough was never in the next prompt. It was on the other side of a walk.

So the last thing in this part is not another technique. It's a permission: **stop. Go touch some grass.** Rest is part of the work. The mind keeps solving after you've closed the laptop. It works quietly underneath, in a way it cannot while you're staring at the cursor demanding an answer. Step away and let it. Then come back and see what it did while you were gone.

This closes the loop the first practice opened. We began with the vision you find by talking; we end with the vision that finds *you* when you finally stop talking. The two are the same circuit. You converse, build, watch, and reflect. Then you rest. Rest is what clears the ground for the next vision to arrive. Walk away from a finished cycle and you come back with the start of the next one.

There's a verse that holds both halves of this without letting go of either: *"see that all these things are done in wisdom and order; for it is not requisite that a man should run faster than he has strength. And again, it is expedient that he should be diligent, that thereby he might win the prize"* ([Mosiah 4:27](../../gospel-library/eng/scriptures/bofm/mosiah/4.md?verse=27)). [qr](../../gospel-library/eng/scriptures/bofm/mosiah/4.md?verse=27) Don't outrun your strength, but don't mistake rest for idleness either. Be diligent *and* don't run faster than you can. Wisdom, and order, and a pace you can keep.

That verse is also the door into the second half of this book. Everything in Part One is *how*: the practices, learned mostly by getting them wrong first. What you just read about stopping, about wisdom and order and not running faster than your strength, is where the *how* opens into the *why*. The rest you've been giving your mind is a small daily rehearsal of something the scriptures treat as the structure of creation itself. If you want to know why these practices work, why counsel precedes creation, why the vision comes first, and why the rest is not optional, turn the page. Part One was the practice. Part Two is the pattern underneath it.

But first: close the laptop. Go outside. The next idea is already on its way. Let it find you.
