## 0. One-paragraph verdict

The published record is consistent on five things. (1) The strongest crossover openings lead with a **concrete scene or a single sharp question**, not with the thesis and not with a definitional throat-clear — and the scene's job is to transfer *authority*, not just to warm the reader up. (2) **Front matter should be short and confessional** ("why this book exists, who I am, what I won't do"); the thesis belongs in the body. (3) A standalone **"How to Read This Book"** section is now widely treated as optional clerical front-matter that short books should fold into the preface or cut — the received wisdom is "don't add pages just because other books have them." (4) **Personal narrative reads as the authority engine when it's placed early** (Newport's Jung tower, Bushman's dialogic stance); an afterword-at-the-back is a deliberate trade that protects the argument's altitude but forfeits that early trust-transfer. (5) A **rigid per-chapter template survives only if the macro-structure is announced once and the variety lives in the content inside each slot** — the failure mode every craft source names is the "blog-post compilation" / "repetitive robot." The cautionary tale for the whole genre is Capra's *The Tao of Physics*: criticized not for drawing parallels but for **advocacy over naming where the parallel breaks down**. *Beyond the Prompt* already does the one thing Capra didn't — it names its own limits (the imago-Dei close, the Ch 6 covenant note). The biggest available wins are at the front door and in the title.

## 1. How do the best crossover / parallel-pattern books OPEN?

### What earns trust in the first five pages

The dominant editorial advice is to open by engaging the reader directly — "pose a question, share a personal experience they can relate to, or pique their interest with a surprising or counterintuitive statement" — with the primary goal of getting the reader "interested enough to move on to the next sentence (and then the next)" ([Penn Editing](https://www.pennediting.com/resources/how-to-write-a-book-introduction-checklist)). Crucially, the same source says do not defer the reader's skepticism: "Don't wait until later chapters to address the reader's objections. If the reader has doubts about your argument at this stage, they won't keep reading" ([Penn Editing](https://www.pennediting.com/resources/how-to-write-a-book-introduction-checklist)). For a dual-audience book, that is a direct mandate — both wary audiences' objections have to be met up front, which is exactly what the current "A note to..." sections attempt.

The creative-nonfiction craft tradition says the same thing in scene terms: "Begin your piece with an intriguing anecdote, vivid description, or provocative question to draw readers in immediately" — the worked example given is a first line that is a concrete moment, not an abstraction ([National Centre for Writing](https://nationalcentreforwriting.org.uk/writing-hub/unlocking-the-art-of-creative-non-fiction-tips-for-writing-compelling-narrative-stories/)).

### Named examples

**C.S. Lewis, *Mere Christianity* — the cold-open question, thesis withheld.** Lewis's preface is exclusively about provenance and posture (the BBC broadcast history, the "popular" tone, who the book is for), and it closes with the hall-and-rooms metaphor: *"It is more like a hall out of which doors open into several rooms. If I can bring anyone into that hall I shall have done what I attempted. But it is in the rooms, not in the hall, that there are fires and chairs and meals"* ([Wise Words](https://wisewordsforyouroccasion.wordpress.com/2022/08/09/mere-christianity-c-s-lewis-preface/)). The thesis is *not argued in the preface*. The actual book opens on a concrete scene — people quarrelling — and reasons up from it. The preface even names the dual audience explicitly: "my unbelieving neighbours," "an outsider" who might be brought into the fold ([Wise Words](https://wisewordsforyouroccasion.wordpress.com/2022/08/09/mere-christianity-c-s-lewis-preface/)). This is the closest formal precedent for *Beyond the Prompt*'s posture: a short confessional preface, the dual audience named, the argument deferred to chapter one.

**Cal Newport, *Deep Work* — the anecdote that transfers authority.** Newport opens not with himself but with Carl Jung building a stone tower at Bollingen to think without distraction — a "historically significant figure who achieved major intellectual breakthroughs through focused work," which "immediately demonstrates that deep work has produced real-world value and credibility" ([Runn summary](https://www.runn.io/blog/deep-work-summary); [Cal Newport's own account of the Jung opening](https://www.calnewport.com/blog/2020/03/22/carl-jungs-fantastical-retreat/)). The lesson for a crossover book: the opening scene's job is to make the parallel *feel inevitable* before any argument is made. *Beyond the Prompt*'s classroom moment (writing "AI" in the margin during a lesson on the spiritual creation) is structurally the same move — a concrete scene that makes the book's central rhyme land before it is defended.

**Yuval Noah Harari, *Nexus* — the single paradoxical question.** Harari opens his prologue with one question: *"We have named our species Homo sapiens, the wise human — but if humans are so wise, why are we doing so many self-destructive things?"* and lets that paradox anchor the entire book's exploration of information networks ([SuperSummary](https://www.supersummary.com/nexus/summary/); [Harari's own prologue notes, PDF](https://www.ynharari.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/Notes-NEXUS-Yuval-Noah-Harari-September-2024.pdf)). A single binding question, asked at the door, does the work of a chapter of setup. *Beyond the Prompt* already uses per-chapter Binding Questions; it does not have one binding question for the whole book stated at the door.

**Terryl & Fiona Givens, *The God Who Weeps* — deference to the skeptic without patronizing.** The book is described as "not defensive, insular, or triumphal," offered "with a kind of deference to a skeptical reader that somehow manages to not sound patronizing," creating "a challenging and ongoing dialogue in honest and mutual respect" ([Dialogue Journal review](https://www.dialoguejournal.com/articles/review-terryl-givens-and-fiona-givens-the-god-who-weeps-how-mormonism-makes-sense-of-life/)). It reaches readers "beyond just the LDS community" by drawing on Western literature and philosophy as shared ground ([Patheos review](https://www.patheos.com/blogs/homewaters/2012/12/review-of-the-god-who-weeps-how-mormonism-makes-sense-of-life-by-terryl-and-fiona-givens.html)). This is the tone target for the two "A note to..." sections: the engineer is not being managed, and the wary Saint is not being scolded.

**Takeaway for the opening.** *Beyond the Prompt* already opens the right way — a concrete scene (the classroom margin) that transfers authority, with the thesis held back. The two refinements the record suggests: (a) consider surfacing **one whole-book binding question** at the door, the way Harari and Lewis do, so the reader carries a single thread through sixteen chapters; (b) make sure the first five pages a skeptical engineer reads actually *meet the objection*, not merely acknowledge it (the current note does acknowledge it well).

## 2. Front matter — how long, what's in it, and the "How to Read This Book" question

### Length and contents in comparable books

The standard distinction the production guides draw: a **preface** is author-written and explains *why the book exists / how it came to be*; an **introduction** is treated as part of the main text and explains *what the book covers and how it's organized*; a **foreword** is third-party and supplies outside credibility ([Kindlepreneur](https://kindlepreneur.com/parts-of-a-book/); [Book Writers Guild](https://bookwritersguild.com/parts-of-a-book/); [Sara Roahen / Chicago Manual conventions](https://www.sararoahen.com/new-blog-1/life-lines-what-are-the-parts-of-a-book-and-where-do-they-go)). A working length target for a modern non-fiction preface is roughly **300–900 words**, up to ~1,200 for memoir-adjacent work, "as long as it stays focused on purpose and reader value" ([Automateed](https://www.automateed.com/book-preface)). *Beyond the Prompt*'s preface at ~30 lines sits inside that target — it is appropriately short.

Lewis's front matter is the model proportion: title + copyright (~2 pp), preface (~5 pp), no introduction, body by roughly page 8 (verified in the archived first pass against [fadedpage.com](https://www.fadedpage.com/books/20150620/html.php)). The repeated editorial caution is against padding: "You don't need to include every possible piece of front matter or back matter just because you've seen it somewhere else... A professional book doesn't need extra pages just to look official. It needs the right pieces in the right places" ([Kindlepreneur](https://kindlepreneur.com/parts-of-a-book/)). One experienced editor goes further on stacking front matter: "You may include all four — foreword, preface, introduction, and author's note — but take care not to wear your reader out before they get to the meat of your book" ([Sara Roahen](https://www.sararoahen.com/new-blog-1/life-lines-what-are-the-parts-of-a-book-and-where-do-they-go)), and admits she finds "most introductions to be filler and/or repetitive, and I often skip them."

### Is a dedicated "How to Read This Book" section a help or a dated crutch?

The received wisdom splits on length. It works in **long, structurally non-obvious books** — danah boyd's *It's Complicated* (a ~250-page academic-trade hybrid) runs an explicit "How to Read This Book" section that tells readers chapters are independent but ordered from individual to societal concerns, and names the broad audience (scholars, parents, educators, journalists) ([It's Complicated, PDF](https://www.danah.org/books/ItsComplicated.pdf)). The justification the production guides give: an explicit roadmap is "particularly important if the content or organization of your book isn't intuitive" ([Penn Editing](https://www.pennediting.com/resources/how-to-write-a-book-introduction-checklist)).

But for **short books, the dedicated section is the kind of clerical front-matter that gets cut.** The consistent modern guidance is to fold the roadmap into the preface/introduction and not give it its own page, and to "leave out anything that only makes the book feel more complicated" ([Kindlepreneur](https://kindlepreneur.com/parts-of-a-book/)). Notably, the current Kindlepreneur "parts of a book" survey doesn't even list a "How to Read This Book" as a standard element — it's not in the canonical inventory of front matter, which is itself a signal that it's an exception, not an expectation.

This validates a decision the project already made: the `00_introduction.md` file (which contained a "How to Read This Book" section) was **deleted on 2026-05-29** because "it prescribed navigation the chapter tags now make self-evident" (per `.mind/active.md`). The research supports that call. The eleven-step reference page + the per-chapter italic tags now carry the navigation, which is the better move for a book this size.

**Takeaway for front matter.** The current shape is close to right: short preface, no separate introduction, no standalone "How to Read This Book." Two refinements: (a) the preface currently carries the dual-audience notes *and* two voice notes — that is four distinct jobs in ~30 lines, which risks the "wear your reader out" problem; consider whether the voice notes can be compressed or moved to a single italic line. (b) The eleven-step reference is doing the roadmap job; make sure nothing in the preface re-explains it (announce once — see §4).

## 3. Where do successful authors place PERSONAL NARRATIVE?

### The three placements and what each costs

**Up front (as authority engine).** Newport tells "the story of my own history with depth, starting with my arrival as a new graduate student at the Theory Group at MIT" *in the introduction* — the personal narrative is "the source of authority, not a memoir aside" (verified in the archived first pass against [calnewport.com](https://calnewport.com/the-book-facebook-doesnt-want-you-to-read/)). Modern preface trends point the same way: authors increasingly "include a 'how this helped me' moment early" and write "with a specific reader in mind" ([Automateed](https://www.automateed.com/book-preface)). The benefit of front placement is immediate trust-transfer; the cost is that backstory up front can drop narrative tension if it runs long — chapter-craft sources warn that "lengthy backstory or information" early "will drop the tension you've created" ([Your Book Starts Here](http://howtoplanwriteanddevelopabook.blogspot.com/2017/10/how-to-build-chapter-cool-new-template.html)).

**Woven throughout.** Bushman's *Rough Stone Rolling* models the "dialogic" stance — he is "fighting on both fronts," explicit that he comes down on his subject's side while presenting enough transparency that a general audience can stay with him ([Deseret profile](https://www.deseret.com/faith/2023/9/21/23836263/richard-bushman-joseph-smith-gold-plates-latter-day-saints-book-of-mormon/); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Bushman)). Woven personal stance keeps the author present without a memoir block; the cost is that it requires discipline to keep the "I" from becoming self-indulgent ("Is there any story included only because it flatters me?" — [Built&Written](https://www.builtwritten.com/blog/nonfiction-book-structure-3-proven-frameworks)).

**Saved for the back (afterword).** Memoir-structure craft is unanimous that you do not lead with your heaviest personal material: "Pace vulnerability carefully — don't lead with your darkest moment. Build trust with readers first" ([River Editor](https://rivereditor.com/guides/how-to-outline-memoir-manuscripts-2026)). An afterword is the maximal version of that pacing.

### What the afterword-at-the-back choice costs and buys *Beyond the Prompt*

The manuscript's choice (the 3.5-year journey lives in Ch 14, the Afterword) is a deliberate trade, and the record clarifies both sides:

- **What it buys:** It keeps the *argument's altitude* high. The book leads with the idea (the eleven-step rhyme) rather than the man, which suits a dual audience — the wary engineer is not asked to care about the author's spiritual biography before being shown that the pattern is real. It also obeys the "earn reflection with scene, scene before reflection" discipline: by the time the reader reaches the Afterword, the chapters have earned the personal payoff. And it protects against the memoir failure mode of front-loaded backstory killing momentum.

- **What it costs:** It forfeits the Newport-style *early authority transfer*. A reader meeting the book cold has a thinner sense of *why this author* until the very end. The preface mitigates this — the classroom moment is a personal scene up front, and the Colophon's "discovered in GitHub Copilot over three and a half years" line plants the credential — but the full arc that would make an engineer think "this person has actually lived inside both worlds for years" is held until after they've decided whether to keep reading.

**Takeaway for personal narrative.** The afterword placement is defensible and the project has good reasons for it. The cheap insurance is to make the **preface's personal scene carry slightly more authority weight** — one or two concrete sentences establishing the dual-life credential (years as a working engineer + active Latter-day Saint) so the cold reader gets the Newport benefit without relocating the whole Afterword. This is a few sentences, not a restructure.

## 4. Keeping a RIGID per-chapter template from feeling formulaic

This is the most actionable area for the second draft, because the book commits to the same five sections sixteen times.

### The named failure mode

Every craft source names the same enemy. Framework books "read like a stack of blog posts, each interesting alone but exhausting in sequence" ([Built&Written](https://www.builtwritten.com/blog/nonfiction-book-structure-3-proven-frameworks)). Template misuse produces "a repetitive robot, your unique expertise flattened" when authors treat the template "like Mad Libs — just plugging in their own words into someone else's sentences"; the fix is to treat templates as "structural guides, not fill-in-the-blank worksheets" ([Ann Sheybani](https://annsheybani.com/why-i-finally-created-book-templates-after-years-of-hating-them/)).

### How successful framework / modular books create variety inside a fixed structure

1. **Announce the macro-structure once; never re-explain it.** This is the single most-repeated rule across the record. Eugene Peterson (*A Long Obedience*) and Marcus Aurelius (*Meditations*) both run identically-scaffolded chapters; the structure is stated once and then disappears so the *content* carries the variety (verified in the archived first pass: [ivpress.com](https://ivpress.com/a-long-obedience-in-the-same-direction); [whitenoise.email](https://www.whitenoise.email/p/why-does-everyone-love-marcus-aurelius)). The corollary warning from that pass: announcing the structure *twice* (e.g., in both an introduction and a reference page) "is the worst of both worlds." *Beyond the Prompt* should announce the five-part template exactly once.

2. **Vary the content inside each slot, not the slot order.** The strongest "same but different" advice: hold the structural skeleton constant and create freshness through "small shifts in structure, context, tone, or theme" ([Writing Excuses 21.05](https://writingexcuses.com/21-05-the-same-but-different/)). The danger is sameness in *small details* — the same opening move, the same kind of example, the same rhythm — not in the macro-beats ([Writing Excuses S3E18](https://writingexcuses.com/writing-excuses-season-3-episode-18-how-to-not-repeat-yourself/)). For *Beyond the Prompt* this means: the Engineering Parallel slot should not always open the same way (a problem statement, then a fix); rotate among a war story, a code artifact, a failure, a definition, a contrast. The manuscript already does some of this — Ch 6 uses `.spec/covenant.yaml` as a physical artifact, Ch 10 uses gardening — but a deliberate inventory of "how does each Engineering Parallel open?" would catch any drift toward one default move.

3. **Vary the texture: not every chapter needs every device at the same weight.** The course-to-book breakdown of Jeff Walker's *Launch* shows a framework book that holds a consistent chapter template while varying which elements appear — "case studies... not in every chapter, but in most"; "numbered lists or #keys... not in every chapter, just where it makes sense" ([Ann Sheybani](https://annsheybani.com/how-to-turn-your-course-into-a-book/)). The fixed template guarantees the reader knows where they are; the *optional* elements inside it create rhythm. *Beyond the Prompt*'s Ch 6 production-note sidebar and Ch 2's non-engineer sidebar are exactly this kind of optional texture — used well, sparingly, they break the metronome without breaking the form.

4. **Use chapter-to-chapter bridges so the template feels like a journey, not a loop.** The "blog-post compilation" cure is a closing bridge: end each chapter "by hinting at the next problem" rather than with a summary ([Built&Written](https://www.builtwritten.com/blog/nonfiction-book-structure-3-proven-frameworks)); chapter-craft calls this the "closing setup" — "they must leave something unresolved... or hint at a new dilemma" ([Your Book Starts Here](http://howtoplanwriteanddevelopabook.blogspot.com/2017/10/how-to-build-chapter-cool-new-template.html)). This is causal *therefore/but* connective tissue between chapters, which is already a stated project principle. The manuscript has added some bridges (Ch 6→7, Ch 8→9 per `.mind/active.md`); the second-draft pass should verify *every* chapter has one, because a fixed template makes a missing bridge feel like a reset.

5. **Let the structure be a writing aid, then loosen the grip.** The deepest craft note: a rigid structure that's "clunky" at first should be internalized until "you no longer even think in terms of the story structure being a separate entity" ([Helping Writers Become Authors](https://www.helpingwritersbecomeauthors.com/formulaic-story-structure/)). Practical version for the edit: allow the five sections to vary in *proportion* chapter to chapter. A chapter where the doctrine is the heavy lift can let the Core Reframe run long and the Engineering Parallel be a single tight illustration; a chapter where the engineering insight is the payload can invert that. Equal-weight sections in every chapter is what reads as mechanical.

**Takeaway for the template.** Keep the five-section template — readers genuinely like knowing where they are, and consistency is what makes the book repurposable and navigable. The variety must live in: (a) how the Engineering Parallel slot *opens* (rotate the move), (b) which optional devices appear (sidebars, lists, artifacts — sparingly), (c) the proportion between sections (don't equalize), and (d) live chapter-to-chapter bridges. Run the second draft with one explicit audit question per slot: *"Does this chapter's [slot] open or move the same way as the last three?"*

## 5. Glossary and Further Reading — placement, length, style

### Glossary

**Placement.** Standard (Chicago Manual of Style) placement is **back matter** — after acknowledgments and any appendices, before endnotes/bibliography/index ([Daniel Tortora](https://danieljtortora.com/blog/glossary-in-a-book); [Turner Proofreading](https://turnerproofreading.com/back-matter-of-a-book/)). Front-matter placement exists only "with the intention being that readers must know the definitions before they are to begin reading" ([Daniel Tortora](https://danieljtortora.com/blog/glossary-in-a-book)); the decision rule is goal-based — front if the reader must learn terms before starting, back if they'll consult as needed ([Academia StackExchange](https://academia.stackexchange.com/questions/118939/where-should-a-glossary-be-placed-in-a-book)). *Beyond the Prompt*'s glossary is in back matter (Ch 15) — correct.

**Style.** The conventions: each term in **bold**, definitions in alphabetical order, one term per line with blank lines between entries, single column, same font as the main text ([Daniel Tortora](https://danieljtortora.com/blog/glossary-in-a-book)). Definitions should be **in the author's own words** (not lifted from a dictionary), should "not try to define every meaning of the term... just define the meaning as the term is used in your book," and should "try not to use other technical terms in defining a term" — aim for what "a high school student with no knowledge of the subject would understand" ([Jessi Hoffman](https://bookeditor-jessihoffman.com/how-to-write-a-book-glossary-nonfiction/)).

**The honesty caution.** A glossary should not be cover for unclear writing: "If your motivation for including a glossary is (partially) to cover up uneven, unclear or incomplete writing, it might be better to revise the text first" ([Edit Better](https://thebetteredit.com/how-to-write-a-glossary/)). And "a glossary is not a substitute for explaining the words at first occurrence in the main text" ([Turner Proofreading](https://turnerproofreading.com/back-matter-of-a-book/)). Also: don't repeat verbatim what the text already said — "Users will be frustrated if they go to the glossary in search of further explanation but only find what they've already read" ([Edit Better](https://thebetteredit.com/how-to-write-a-glossary/)). Final check: "Is every term in the glossary actually used in the text?" ([Edit Better](https://thebetteredit.com/how-to-write-a-glossary/)).

**Note on the current glossary's two-part design.** Ch 15 has two sections: "Fused Terms" (scripture concept → engineering equivalent) and "Plain-English Technical Terms" (for non-engineers). This is unusual and *good* — it serves the dual audience structurally, mapping in both directions. The craft caution to apply: the two sections risk inconsistent definition-depth and risk re-stating chapter content. The second-draft pass should (a) verify every glossed term actually appears in the body, and (b) keep the plain-English definitions free of *other* technical terms (a few currently reach for "neural network," "vector space" inside other entries — acceptable only if each is itself glossed, which they are).

### Further Reading

**Placement and form.** Further-reading / recommended-study lists live in back matter and are explicitly endorsed as one of the best uses of "the end of the book" — a place to "deepen trust, extend usefulness, and create the reader's next step," not "dead space" ([Kindlepreneur](https://kindlepreneur.com/parts-of-a-book/); [Book Writers Guild](https://bookwritersguild.com/parts-of-a-book/)). The annotated form *Beyond the Prompt* uses (each entry with a one-line description of what it offers) is the higher-value version — it functions as curation, not just citation, which strengthens authority ([Edit Better's principle that original framing "adds value" over generic lists](https://thebetteredit.com/how-to-write-a-glossary/) applies by extension).

**Style note for the current list.** Ch 16's three-tier structure (Scriptural Anchors / Prophetic Messages / Deep-Dive Studies, the last QR-coded to cpuchip.net) is well-formed. The one craft caution: keep entry descriptions roughly even in length and information-density — the editor's-eye rule for any back-matter list is consistency, and the same discipline that governs glossary entries ("rewrite any definitions significantly more or less detailed than the others," [Daniel Tortora](https://danieljtortora.com/blog/glossary-in-a-book)) applies here.

**Takeaway for back matter.** Placement and form are both correct and above-average (the two-direction glossary and the annotated/QR further-reading are genuine strengths). The work is verification, not redesign: every glossed term used in the body, no glossary entry that merely repeats the chapter, even description lengths.

## 6. Title / subtitle craft — does the current title do its job?

### The current title against the received wisdom

**"Beyond the Prompt: What AI Engineering Reveals About Eternal Patterns"**

The dominant non-fiction title pattern is: **an evocative/intriguing main title + a clarifying subtitle that names the topic, the audience, and the payoff** ([Listenable](https://listenable.io/web/articles/lessons/7b84c3aa/2312/the-structure-of-a-book-title/); [FriesenPress](https://www.friesenpress.com/blog/2019/5/30/the-winning-combination-how-a-gregarious-title-and-scrupulous-subtitle-win-you-readers)). The title "grabs the gut," the subtitle "closes the deal" by clearly expressing what the book does for the reader ([Jane Friedman / Rein & Larsen](https://janefriedman.com/how-to-title-your-nonfiction-book/); [FriesenPress](https://www.friesenpress.com/blog/2019/5/30/the-winning-combination-how-a-gregarious-title-and-scrupulous-subtitle-win-you-readers)). The colon-subtitle convention is standard for exactly this purpose ([Writer's Digest on colons](https://www.writersdigest.com/golden-rules-for-colon-usage)).

**What the current title does well.** *"Beyond the Prompt"* is a strong, short, brandable main title — it's intriguing, it signals AI to the engineer, and "Beyond" hints at depth past the surface tool. It satisfies the "short, evocative main title" half cleanly.

**Where it's weak, measured against the craft rules:**

1. **The subtitle doesn't name the second audience or the payoff for either.** Strong subtitles "clarify who the book's ideal readers are" and "showcase the book's benefits to the reader" ([Greenleaf](https://greenleafbookgroup.com/learning-center/book-creation/titling-best-practices-how-to-reel-readers-in-with-book-titles-that-are-optimized-for-the-web)). *"What AI Engineering Reveals About Eternal Patterns"* tells the reader the *mechanism* (AI engineering reveals patterns) but not *who it's for* or *what they get*. A faithful Latter-day Saint scanning a shelf doesn't see themselves; the word "eternal patterns" is doing the religious signaling but it's soft. An engineer sees "AI Engineering" but no payoff.

2. **"Eternal Patterns" is the clever-but-ambiguous risk the title literature warns about.** The repeated caution: "Don't be clever — unless you use an obvious subtitle"; a personal or abstract phrase "could be misunderstood by readers who aren't familiar with the term," and the fix is "a clear, targeted subtitle" ([Creative Penn](https://creativepennbooks.com/blogs/how-to-write-non-fiction-2nd-edition/1-7)). The canonical example is *Irresistible*, which "could be a dessert cookbook, a romance novel, or something on how to attract a partner" until the subtitle disambiguates ([Creative Penn](https://creativepennbooks.com/blogs/how-to-write-non-fiction-2nd-edition/1-7)). "Eternal Patterns" is similarly soft — it could read as new-age, self-help, or pattern-language software. The subtitle is currently the *only* place the religious frame lands, and it lands obliquely.

3. **Searchability/keywords.** Subtitles are where target-audience keywords belong ([Indigo Editing](https://indigoediting.com/writing-for-eyeballs-and-algorithms-how-to-create-titles-and-subtitles-that-work-for-humans-and-seo/); [Greenleaf](https://greenleafbookgroup.com/learning-center/book-creation/titling-best-practices-how-to-reel-readers-in-with-book-titles-that-are-optimized-for-the-web)). The current subtitle contains "AI Engineering" (good) but no faith keyword that a Latter-day Saint or a "faith and technology" reader would search — "Creation," "Scripture," "Christian," "Latter-day Saint," "faith" are all absent.

### What comparable successful titles do that this could learn from

- **Name both halves of the crossover in the subtitle.** *The God Who Weeps* (evocative title) → *How Mormonism Makes Sense of Life* (names the tradition and the payoff). The Givens subtitle tells you exactly which faith and what it does for you ([Goodreads](https://www.goodreads.com/en/book/show/15954244)).
- **Let the subtitle carry the explicit topic the title only hints at.** *Seth Godin's All Marketers Are Liars* needed *The Power of Telling Authentic Stories in a Low-Trust World* to disambiguate — and the misunderstanding was bad enough that Godin retitled the book ([Creative Penn](https://creativepennbooks.com/blogs/how-to-write-non-fiction-2nd-edition/1-7)). A cautionary parallel: if "Eternal Patterns" reads ambiguously, the subtitle has to over-correct toward clarity.
- **Short evocative title + descriptive subtitle is the proven crossover combo:** *Freakonomics* and *POP!* both pair an invented/odd memorable title with a precise, longer subtitle that "closes the deal" ([FriesenPress](https://www.friesenpress.com/blog/2019/5/30/the-winning-combination-how-a-gregarious-title-and-scrupulous-subtitle-win-you-readers)).

**Takeaway for the title.** Keep *"Beyond the Prompt."* It's the strongest asset in the title. The subtitle is where the work is. Test alternatives that (a) name the faith frame explicitly enough that a Latter-day Saint sees themselves, (b) name the engineer, and (c) state a payoff. The "title reconsideration" item is already flagged as a Tier 4.x sequel-material task in `.mind/active.md`; this research argues it should be promoted, because the title/subtitle is the highest-leverage single sentence in the book for a dual audience — it is the only thing both wary readers see before deciding.

*Illustrative directions (not finished copy — for the title session to react to):*
- *Beyond the Prompt: How the Work of Building AI Reveals the Pattern of How God Builds Us* — names both audiences (build AI / God builds us), states the rhyme as payoff.
- *Beyond the Prompt: An Engineer's Discovery of the Creation Pattern in Modern AI* — names the engineer, names the scriptural frame ("Creation"), keeps "discovery" honest to the book's own posture.
- *Beyond the Prompt: What Software Engineering and the Scriptures Say to Each Other* — names both halves explicitly, dialogic tone matching the Givens model.

## 7. FIVE prioritized recommendations for *Beyond the Prompt*

**1. Rework the subtitle (highest leverage, lowest effort).** Keep "Beyond the Prompt." Replace the current subtitle with one that names *both* audiences and a payoff, and that surfaces a faith keyword for discoverability. The current "What AI Engineering Reveals About Eternal Patterns" is clever-but-soft on the religious half and names no reader and no benefit — the exact failure the title literature warns about ([Creative Penn](https://creativepennbooks.com/blogs/how-to-write-non-fiction-2nd-edition/1-7); [Greenleaf](https://greenleafbookgroup.com/learning-center/book-creation/titling-best-practices-how-to-reel-readers-in-with-book-titles-that-are-optimized-for-the-web)). This single sentence is the only thing both wary readers see before deciding. Promote the title task out of Tier 4. *(Grounded in §6.)*

**2. Run a "template-fatigue audit" on the second draft, slot by slot.** For each of the five sections, ask: *does it open or move the same way in three consecutive chapters?* — focusing on the Engineering Parallel slot, which is most at risk of defaulting to "problem → fix." Rotate the opening move (war story / artifact / failure / contrast / definition) and let section proportions vary chapter to chapter rather than equalizing. This is the single biggest threat to a 16x-fixed-template book and the most-cited failure mode in the craft record ([Built&Written](https://www.builtwritten.com/blog/nonfiction-book-structure-3-proven-frameworks); [Writing Excuses 21.05](https://writingexcuses.com/21-05-the-same-but-different/); [Ann Sheybani](https://annsheybani.com/why-i-finally-created-book-templates-after-years-of-hating-them/)). *(Grounded in §4.)*

**3. Verify a live chapter-to-chapter bridge on every chapter.** A fixed template makes a missing bridge read as a reset, turning the book into a "blog-post compilation." Each chapter (except the last) should end by handing forward — causally, *therefore/but* — to the next, not with a summary ([Built&Written](https://www.builtwritten.com/blog/nonfiction-book-structure-3-proven-frameworks); [Your Book Starts Here](http://howtoplanwriteanddevelopabook.blogspot.com/2017/10/how-to-build-chapter-cool-new-template.html)). The project has added some; the second draft should confirm *all* sixteen. This also satisfies the workspace's own Therefore/But-not-And-Then principle at the chapter scale. *(Grounded in §4.)*

**4. Add one or two authority-bearing sentences to the preface's personal scene.** The afterword-at-the-back choice is sound, but it forfeits Newport-style early trust-transfer. Insure cheaply: in the preface's classroom moment, plant the dual-life credential (working engineer + active Latter-day Saint, years of it) so a cold reader gets "this person has lived in both worlds" before deciding to continue — without relocating the Afterword ([Runn / Newport's Jung opening](https://www.runn.io/blog/deep-work-summary); [Automateed on the early "how this helped me" moment](https://www.automateed.com/book-preface)). *(Grounded in §3.)*

**5. Do a glossary/back-matter verification pass (not a redesign).** The two-direction glossary and the annotated, QR-coded further-reading are genuine strengths — leave the design. Verify: (a) every glossed term actually appears in the body ([Edit Better](https://thebetteredit.com/how-to-write-a-glossary/)); (b) no glossary entry merely repeats chapter prose ([Edit Better](https://thebetteredit.com/how-to-write-a-glossary/)); (c) plain-English definitions don't lean on un-glossed technical terms ([Jessi Hoffman](https://bookeditor-jessihoffman.com/how-to-write-a-book-glossary-nonfiction/)); (d) further-reading entry descriptions are even in length/density ([Daniel Tortora](https://danieljtortora.com/blog/glossary-in-a-book)). This closes the still-open glossary provenance gap noted in `.mind/active.md`. *(Grounded in §5.)*

## 8. The genre's central trap — and the news that the book already avoids it

The cautionary tale for every parallel-pattern crossover book is Fritjof Capra's *The Tao of Physics*. The academic critique is precise and worth holding in view: Capra is faulted not for drawing parallels between physics and Eastern mysticism but for **advocacy over rigor — for failing "to take account of the pitfalls associated with drawing parallels"** and for not justifying where the analogy holds versus where it collapses ([Springer, "The Pitfalls of Parallelism"](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-94-009-7058-8_2); [Restivo, "Parallels and Paradoxes," SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/030631277800800201)). The sharpest version: critics charged that "the parallels prove even less than does the fact that both cows and kitchen chairs have 4 legs," and that the genre "emphasized advocacy over rigorous critical analysis of where and why these analogies actually break down" (summarized from the [Restivo critique](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/030631282012001003)).

*Beyond the Prompt* already does the thing Capra didn't. The per-chapter imago-Dei close, the Ch 6 passage that explicitly stops the parallel ("These are tools failing under law, not souls falling under sin. A machine's failure can illustrate a doctrine; it cannot commit one"), and the engineer-facing preface note ("Where the patterns rhyme, take them. Where they do not, the book names the limit") are the structural antibody to the Capra failure. The second-draft edit should *protect* this — it is the book's strongest credibility move with the skeptical engineer, and the temptation in revision is always to soften a limit into a hedge or drop it for flow. Keep the limits sharp and explicit; they are not weaknesses in the argument, they are the proof that the method is honest.
