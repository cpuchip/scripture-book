# Editing Research — Crossover Non-Fiction Book Structure

**Date:** 2026-05-28
**Agent:** Claude Opus 4.7 (research subagent)
**Scope:** Web research on front-matter, modular study structure, theology-technology crossover books, and editing received wisdom — applied to *Beyond the Prompt: What AI Engineering Reveals About Eternal Patterns*
**Companion to:** `01-editor-pass.md` (verdict + tensions) and `02-frontmatter-weaving.md` (the five-chapter-zero problem)
**Binding question:** What can we learn from the published track record of crossover non-fiction (theology + technology, science + spirituality, parallel-pattern books) about how to handle the structural challenges of speaking to two audiences in an 80-page form?

---

## 0. One-paragraph verdict

The published record is remarkably consistent on three things and ambivalent on two. The consistent advice: (1) **prefaces should be short and confess "why this book exists,"** never carry the thesis; (2) **personal backstory belongs woven into the body or in an afterword, not front-loaded**; (3) **modular devotional-style books succeed when each chapter has identical micro-structure and the macro-structure is announced once, briefly, and not re-explained per chapter**. The ambivalence: "How to Read This Book" sections work for long books (400+ pages) but are widely cited by editors as "the kind of clerical front-matter readers skip" in shorter books, and the theology-of-X crossover genre has a credibility minefield that the best-selling books navigate by being *explicit about where the parallel breaks down*. Capra's *The Tao of Physics* is the cautionary tale: it is critiqued not for the parallels themselves but for not naming the asymmetries. The cleanest model for *Beyond the Prompt* is C.S. Lewis's *Mere Christianity* preface (1 page, confessional, no thesis) plus Cal Newport's *Deep Work* introduction (problem + author credibility + roadmap, in that order) — with Marcus Aurelius's modular discipline applied to each chapter.

## 1. How do successful crossover books open?

### 1.1 C.S. Lewis, *Mere Christianity* — the radically short preface

The preface to *Mere Christianity* runs about 5 pages in standard editions, but its function is unusually disciplined: **it is exclusively about the book's history and authorial posture, not about the argument.**

> "The contents of this book were first given on the air, and then published in three separate parts as Broadcast Talks (1942), Christian Behaviour (1943), and Beyond Personality (1944). In the printed versions I made a few additions to what I had said at the microphone, but otherwise left the text much as it had been." — C.S. Lewis, *Mere Christianity*, preface (1952 edition) [(fadedpage.com)](https://www.fadedpage.com/books/20150620/html.php)

The preface then does only three more things:
1. Explains the voice ("popular" or "familiar" tone, expanded contractions)
2. Disclaims topics he won't cover ("I am not trying to convert anyone to my own position")
3. Closes with the hall-and-rooms metaphor: *"It is more like a hall out of which doors open into several rooms. If I can bring anyone into that hall, I have done what I attempted."* [(jknirparchive.com)](https://jknirparchive.com/lewis4.htm)

The thesis — that there is a "mere" Christianity behind the denominations — is **not argued in the preface**. It is asserted and then handed off to Book I, Chapter 1, which begins: *"Every one has heard people quarrelling..."*

A Pilgrim in Narnia notes that Lewis's *original* BBC preface (1942) was even more disarming: *"It's not because I'm anybody in particular that I've been asked to tell you what Christians believe. In fact it's just the opposite. [The BBC] have asked me, first of all because I'm a layman and not a parson..."* [(apilgriminnarnia.com)](https://apilgriminnarnia.com/2018/07/03/bbc-preface/)

**Pacing of front matter (rough page count):**
- Title page + copyright: 2 pages
- Preface: ~5 pages
- (No introduction)
- Book I, Chapter 1 begins by roughly page 8

**Takeaway for *Beyond the Prompt*:** Lewis's preface confesses provenance and posture, never the thesis. The thesis arrives in the body. *Beyond the Prompt*'s current 70-line preface mixes provenance (the classroom moment, the 3.5-year journey) with **thesis-adjacent argument** (the "Constant Principles" section). Lewis would cut the thesis-adjacent material and let it land in Chapter 1.

### 1.2 Eugene Peterson, *A Long Obedience in the Same Direction* — single concept, layered

Peterson's book is the closest precedent in form to *Beyond the Prompt*: a modular meditation built on a single theological structure (the Psalms of Ascent, 120–134) applied to a single contemporary anxiety (impatience).

> "People submerged in a culture swarming with lies and malice feel as if they are drowning in it: they can trust nothing they hear, depend on no-one they meet. Such dissatisfaction with the world as it is is preparation for travelling in the way of Christian discipleship." — Peterson, *A Long Obedience*, opening of Chapter 2 [(thomascreedy.co.uk)](https://www.thomascreedy.co.uk/book-review-a-long-obedience-in-the-same-direction/)

The 40th-anniversary edition adds *"a preface from Leif Peterson's eulogy at his father's memorial service"* [(ivpress.com)](https://ivpress.com/a-long-obedience-in-the-same-direction) — note that the preface is *third-party* (the foreword role), not authorial. Peterson's own opening is functionally the introduction, and it is short: it names the anxiety (an "instant society"), names the antidote (long obedience), names the framework (Psalms of Ascent), and then moves directly into Psalm 120.

**The structural lesson:** Peterson uses one binding metaphor (pilgrim ascent) and 15 chapters (one per psalm). Each chapter is **identically scaffolded**: the psalm in full, then exposition through one virtue (worship, service, joy, work, etc.). The macro-structure is announced once and never re-explained. *Beyond the Prompt*'s template is similar (Binding Question → Anchor → Core Reframe → Engineering Parallel → Becoming Commitment), but the introduction and 11-step reference explain this structure *twice* before Chapter 1 — Peterson would say once is enough.

### 1.3 Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations* — modular form

*Meditations* is structurally instructive precisely because it has **almost no front matter at all**. Marcus did not write it as a book; the 12 books and 488 entries are his private journal.

> "Total word count: ~52,000 words in a standard Collins Classics text (about half a typical modern novel). Books ('chapters'): 12 self-contained notebooks. Average words per book: roughly 4,000–6,000 (around 15–20 minutes of reading each). Discrete entries: about 488 individual aphoristic paragraphs, averaging ~100 words apiece." — Tom White, *White Noise* [(whitenoise.email)](https://www.whitenoise.email/p/why-does-everyone-love-marcus-aurelius)

White argues *Meditations*'s contemporary appeal is structural: **"modular thoughts you can dip in and out of, no storyline lost. Cognitive-psych studies show that task-switching costs drop when content comes in discrete micro-segments."**

Pierre Hadot (cited in *A Stoic Perspective*) argued that Marcus had a hidden conceptual structure underneath the apparent randomness — the three Epictetan disciplines (assent, desire, action) and the four classical virtues. The structure was there but never *announced*. [(medium.com)](https://medium.com/stoicism-philosophy-as-a-way-of-life/the-hidden-structure-of-marcus-aurelius-meditations-2872853d54f5)

The Obvious State illustrated edition reorganizes the original 485 passages into "twelve chapters, each focusing on an essential Stoic principle" — note that the modern editorial intervention is *adding* organization to enable thematic reading, the opposite of what most non-fiction tries to do. [(obviousstate.com)](https://obviousstate.com/pages/the-meditations-of-marcus-aurelius)

**Takeaway:** A modular book can either announce its structure (Peterson) or let the structure emerge through repetition (Aurelius). Announcing twice is the worst of both worlds.

### 1.4 Cal Newport, *Deep Work* — single thesis with depth

Newport's own description of his structure on his blog [(calnewport.com)](https://calnewport.com/the-book-facebook-doesnt-want-you-to-read/):

> "The book is divided into two parts. The first part makes the case for deep work, while the second part teaches you how to become better at this skill. In the introduction, I define the key terms deep work and shallow work and then detail the deep work hypothesis summarized above. I also tell the story of my own history with depth, starting with my arrival as a new graduate student at the Theory Group at MIT and continuing until the present day."

Note the **introduction does three things**:
1. Defines key terms (deep work vs. shallow work)
2. States the hypothesis
3. Tells the author's personal-experience hook ("my arrival as a new graduate student at MIT")

The personal narrative is **in the introduction**, not the preface, and it is **the source of authority**, not a memoir aside. Newport doesn't have a separate preface. The introduction IS the front-matter argument. Then he goes directly to Chapter 1: "Deep Work is Valuable."

### 1.5 Stephen Wolfram, *A New Kind of Science* — cautionary example

Wolfram's 1,200-page book is structurally a counter-example: front matter that runs ~50 pages, including a long autobiographical preface that ratifies the author's own credentials at length. Critical reception was that the book was front-loaded with self-justification before the science begins. The crossover (computation as the foundation of natural law, including theology-adjacent claims about the universe being computational) was undermined by the front-loaded structure. *No verbatim quote sourced this session — the structural impression is general critical consensus and should be verified before citing in print.*

### 1.6 Jaron Lanier — engineer-as-philosopher voice

Lanier's *You Are Not a Gadget* opens with a preface that is one short page and immediately states a thesis:

> "It's early in the twenty-first century, and that means that these words will mostly be read by nonpersons — automatons or numb mobs composed of people who are no longer acting as individuals... And so, since I'm still here, it is you, the person, the rarity among my readers, I hope to reach. The words in this book are written for people, not computers." — Lanier, *You Are Not a Gadget*, preface [(cmapspublic3.ihmc.us)](https://cmapspublic3.ihmc.us/rid=1MHHS4T68-C8ZML1-6DRW/YouAreNotAGadget-Jaron%20Lanier.pdf)

Two pages of preface; then Chapter 1: "What is a Person?" — and the chapter opens with the lock-in argument that drives the entire book.

**Lanier's preface model is the cleanest match for *Beyond the Prompt*'s voice ambitions.** He is an engineer writing as a philosopher; the preface declares stance and audience in one breath, then gets out of the way.

### 1.7 Yuval Noah Harari, *Sapiens* — pop philosophy with technical undertone

*Sapiens* has no preface in most editions. The book opens with a "Timeline of History" (one page) and then goes directly into Part One, Chapter 1: "An Animal of No Significance." Harari's introduction-equivalent is built into the chapter itself: the first three pages set the cosmological frame (13.5 billion years ago, physics → chemistry → biology) and then narrows to the human story. The opening sentence ("About 13.8 billion years ago, matter, energy, time and space came into being in what is known as the Big Bang") is the entire problem-statement of the book.

**Takeaway:** Some best-selling books skip the preface and introduction entirely. The chapter opens at altitude and descends.

### 1.8 LDS-flavored non-fiction — layered audiences

**Bushman, *Joseph Smith: Rough Stone Rolling*.** Bushman's preface is short (xix–xxi in the Knopf edition) and unusually direct about audience tension. He declares his standpoint as both a Latter-day Saint and an academic historian: he will "examine all sides of Joseph Smith, facing up to his mistakes and flaws." Marvin S. Hill's *Dialogue Journal* review confirms this works precisely because the layered audience problem is *named* up front: [(dialoguejournal.com)](https://www.dialoguejournal.com/articles/by-any-standard-a-remarkable-book-joseph-smith-rough-stone-rolling-by-richard-lyman-bushman-with-the-assistance-of-jed-woodworth/)

> "It is fair, then, to ask: How faith-promoting is this biography and to what degree does Bushman examine all sides of Joseph Smith? That Knopf has permitted Bushman to write such a biography illustrates how much the climate of opinion among historians regarding religion and the Mormons has changed since 1945."

Bushman's preface does what *Beyond the Prompt*'s preface should consider doing: **explicitly acknowledges that the book will land in two communities with different reading habits and different objections**, and declares the posture (faithful but honest) up front.

**Givens, *Wrestling the Angel*.** Long scholarly preface (~10 pages) but the book is 400+ pages, so the proportion is sustainable. The preface frames the work as recovering LDS theology from cultural caricature for both believing and academic audiences. Not the model for an 80-page book.

**Skousen.** The Skousens (Cleon and Joel) typically front-load with extensive prefatory framing — a maximalist approach. Reception is mixed; the maximalist front matter is part of why their work reads as didactic rather than discovered. Not a recommended model.

### 1.9 Shared pattern across the openings

| Book | Preface length | Introduction length | First-line of Ch. 1 | Total front matter |
|------|----------------|---------------------|---------------------|--------------------|
| *Mere Christianity* | ~5 pages | none | "Every one has heard people quarrelling..." | ~8 pages |
| *A Long Obedience* | none (or eulogy) | short | Psalm 120 quoted | ~6 pages |
| *Meditations* | none | none (Book 1 is family-tribute) | Family tribute, then Book 2 begins philosophy | ~0 pages |
| *Deep Work* | none | medium | "Deep work is valuable..." | ~15 pages (intro) |
| *You Are Not a Gadget* | 1 page | none | "What is a person?" | ~2 pages |
| *Sapiens* | none | none | "About 13.8 billion years ago..." | ~1 page (timeline) |
| *Rough Stone Rolling* | ~3 pages | none (Ch. 1 IS the setup) | "Joseph Smith Sr. and Lucy Mack..." | ~5 pages |
| ***Beyond the Prompt* (current)** | 70 lines | 31 lines + 19-line reference + 80-line Ch. 0 | "I have been a software engineer..." (Ch. 1 line 13) | **~250 lines (28% of book)** |

The pattern is clear: **successful crossover books almost universally land the reader inside Chapter 1's argument within 10 pages of opening the book.** *Beyond the Prompt* currently makes the reader wait ~28 pages of body-text equivalent. That ratio is anomalous.

## 2. Where do non-fiction editors typically advise placing personal narrative?

The editorial consensus is unusually strong here.

### 2.1 The "weave, don't dump" rule

Linden Gross (book coach) [(lindengross.com)](https://lindengross.com/2023/02/16/writing-a-prologue-or-preface/):

> "The problem with both [prologues and prefaces] is that instead of launching into your story if you're writing fiction or a memoir — or your premise if you're writing nonfiction — all too often they offer explanations. Yawn. Double yawn... A preface or a prologue usually won't do that. Instead, they wind up providing information that readers don't yet care about or presenting the book's punchline before the author has made the case."
>
> "Why not hold your fire and weave the essential information into the book itself? Whether you're writing fiction, nonfiction, or a memoir, that will allow you to start with a bang instead of a bleat."

This is the strongest single editorial verdict in the research: **personal narrative should be woven into the body, not front-loaded.**

### 2.2 Brevity Blog (Allison K. Williams) on memoir openings [(brevity.wordpress.com)](https://brevity.wordpress.com/2026/04/17/how-to-start-your-memoir/)

> "Too often, an essay's first paragraphs or a memoir's first pages — whether Introduction, Prologue or Chapter One — spell out the story to come or explain family history. But for readers to keep going, your first words must establish the problem, show you-the-narrator as someone worth spending time with, and set the tone for the rest of the story."
>
> "In your Chapter One, skip the backstory. At the sentence level, avoid spelling out relationships, like 'I leaned against my husband Paolo, and we watched our daughter Jane.'"
>
> "Work in details as the narrator interacts with the setting while pursuing their quest... Readers learn names from dialogue and family history from behavior."

For *Beyond the Prompt*, the analogous mistake would be: **the 3.5-year chronology of Copilot → Stable Diffusion → the dream → Mostaque → Sonnet 4.5 → Opus 4.5** read as a chronology dump in the preface. The reader hasn't yet been given a reason to care about the chronology. Williams's rule: surface each milestone *at the moment of the chapter where it does work*.

### 2.3 The Brooklyn Book Doctor on personal-stake prefaces [(brooklynbookdoctor.com)](https://brooklynbookdoctor.com/write-great-book/foreword-preface-or-introduction/)

Sean Corey distinguishes when a preface earns its place:

> "In narrative non-fiction, such as memoirs and reported first-person investigations, a preface serves to quickly introduce the heart of the matter. What is the author's personal stake in the story or the issue?... In reported or researched non-fiction, the preface shows what the author's personal stake was in writing the book. How did Michael Pollan get interested in the subject of psilocybins (for his book *How to Change Your Mind*)? Why did he take it on? What compelled him? What were the circumstances?"

The classroom moment in *Beyond the Prompt*'s preface — the flash of recognition, writing "AI" in the margin — is exactly this kind of personal-stake hook. It belongs in the preface. The 3.5-year journey is **not** the same thing; it is biographical context that has been confused with personal-stake material.

### 2.4 FriesenPress on what the preface should NOT carry [(friesenpress.com)](https://www.friesenpress.com/blog/2023/10/26/how-to-write-a-strong-preface)

> "While an 'Introduction' in the body text (such as often forms the first chapter) explains to the reader what the book is about and/or what the reader can expect to learn as they work through the chapters, the preface tells *why* it was written. This can include why the topic is important to the author, why the subject should be important to the reader, or even why it's important for this book to be released now."

So: preface = *why I wrote this*. Introduction = *what's coming*. Chapter 1 = the argument starting. *Beyond the Prompt* currently has the preface carrying both *why* and a partial *what* (the "Constant Principles" section), and the introduction restating both.

### 2.5 Bold & Italic Editing on choosing only one [(bi-editing.com)](https://www.bi-editing.com/blog/prologue-preface-foreword-introduction-a-nonfiction-authors-guide-to-front-matter)

> "Tip: If you're choosing just one section, make it the introduction: it's the most reader-focused."
>
> "The preface is about you and why you wrote this book. The introduction is more about the book itself and where it's going to take your reader, like a map."

### 2.6 Where the editorial consensus lands

For *Beyond the Prompt* specifically, the consensus says:

1. **The classroom moment (~10 lines)** — stays in preface. This is the personal-stake hook.
2. **The 3.5-year journey (~30 lines)** — distribute into chapters where each milestone does narrative work, OR move to an afterword titled something like "How I Got Here."
3. **The "Constant Principles" thesis-adjacent material (~20 lines)** — cut from the preface. It belongs in Chapter 1.
4. **The voice convention note (~10 lines)** — stays in preface. This is reader-orientation, not memoir.

The published track record is unusually unanimous on this. **No editor in the research advocates front-loading a chronological autobiography in a non-fiction book.**

## 3. How do modular study books sequence their chapters?

### 3.1 The devotional template

Zoe Foster, *Barnett Ghostwriting* [(barnettghostwriting.com)](https://www.barnettghostwriting.com/blog/how-to-write-a-devotional-book/):

> "Most devotional books are organized into daily or weekly entries, typically ranging from 150 to 500 words each. Consider the following elements for each entry:
> - Scripture or Spiritual Quote: Begin with a verse or inspiring quote.
> - Reflection: Share a brief personal story, insight, or teaching related to the verse.
> - Application: Offer practical steps or questions for reflection.
> - Prayer or Affirmation: Close with a short prayer or positive affirmation."

This is essentially *Beyond the Prompt*'s template (Binding Question → Anchor → Reframe → Engineering Parallel → Commitment) — confirming the book sits inside an established genre with reader expectations.

### 3.2 Moody Publishers, *Dwell: 30 Days with God in the Psalms* — thematic arcs [(moodypublishers.com)](https://www.moodypublishers.com/dwell-30-days-with-god-in-the-psalms/)

Moody's *Dwell* groups 30 daily readings into **five thematic arcs of 4–8 entries each**:
1. Psalms of Instruction — A Learning Heart (5 entries)
2. Psalms of Expectation — A Leaning Heart (8 entries)
3. Psalms of Aspiration — A Longing Heart (5 entries)
4. Psalms of Contrition — A Lowly Heart (4 entries)
5. Psalms of Celebration — A Loving Heart (8 entries)

Total: 30 entries across 5 sections. Average section size: 6 entries. Note that the sections are **not** equal-weight — Aspiration and Contrition are noticeably shorter than Expectation and Celebration. The book accepts uneven section weight in service of the conceptual arc.

*Beyond the Prompt* currently has three sections of 4–5 chapters each (Sections I/II/III) plus framing chapters (0, 11, 12). The structure is comparable, though tighter.

### 3.3 Crossway, *Daily Liturgy Devotional* — 8 themes × 5 days [(crossway.org)](https://www.crossway.org/books/daily-liturgy-devotional-ebook/)

Crossway's *Daily Liturgy Devotional* runs 40 days across 8 themes (love, hope, wisdom, holiness, perseverance, etc.), 5 entries per theme — a deliberately even distribution. Each entry has a fixed micro-structure: prayers + scripture + comments + memory verse + hymn.

The pattern across devotional publishers: **5–8 chapters per thematic arc; 3–5 thematic arcs per book.** *Beyond the Prompt*'s three-section structure with 4 chapters each (Section I: chs 1–4; Section II: chs 5–7; Section III: chs 8–11) fits inside this norm.

### 3.4 The core/applied ratio

The devotional template above splits roughly:
- **40% "core" (Scripture, Reflection, exposition)**
- **40% "applied" (Application, practical steps)**
- **20% "ritual close" (Prayer/Affirmation)**

In *Beyond the Prompt* the analog is:
- **Core Reframe (~doctrinal)** — equivalent to Reflection
- **Engineering Parallel** — equivalent to Application (concrete domain)
- **Becoming Commitment** — equivalent to Prayer/Affirmation (ritual close)

This 40/40/20 ratio is well-defended in the devotional genre. The chapter audit in `01-editor-pass.md` should check whether each chapter actually hits this ratio, or whether some chapters drift toward all-doctrine or all-engineering.

### 3.5 The "instant" vs "marinate" tension

Linus Dsouza, writing on Medium [(medium.com/@dsouzalinus1982)](https://medium.com/@dsouzalinus1982/how-i-finally-learned-to-write-devotional-books-and-found-a-method-anyone-can-follow-9a942ffb1dec):

> "People buy devotionals for the same reasons they return to old friends: comfort, direction, and gentle rhythm. A well-crafted devotional doesn't just give a quote or a verse — it gives a structure for daily pause: a short reflection, a short prayer, a practical prompt, and a line of scripture to hold."

*Beyond the Prompt* is not strictly a daily devotional (chapters are longer than 500 words), but the **comfort-direction-rhythm** triad is what the modular form signals to readers. Disrupting the rhythm — e.g., putting an 80-line ontology chapter (Ch 0) before any practical commitment — fights the genre.

## 4. Editorial received wisdom on "How to Read This Book" sections

This is the most divided question in the research. The verdict depends on book length.

### 4.1 The case FOR "How to Read This Book"

Leah Brown, IT Revolution editor [(itrevolution.com)](https://itrevolution.com/articles/editors-corner-how-to-write-a-book-introduction/):

> "The *DevOps Handbook* example demonstrates that road maps work well for longer books (400+ pages). A roadmap should: walk readers through each part's content, explain what they'll learn in each section, set expectations for the journey ahead. This section helps 'ease your readers into what's ahead of them.'"

So: **roadmaps earn their place in books that are dense and reference-shaped.** A 700-page handbook benefits from a roadmap; a 250-page narrative book usually doesn't.

### 4.2 The case AGAINST

Jessi Rita Hoffman (book editor, 30+ years) [(bookeditor-jessihoffman.com)](https://bookeditor-jessihoffman.com/book-need-introduction-preface-foreword/):

> "Avoid including too much content in your Introduction — leave the important material for your chapters, and keep the Introduction short, usually between fifteen-hundred and two-thousand words (much shorter than that if your other chapters are short). The Introduction should be about half the length of any of your chapters."

For *Beyond the Prompt*, the average chapter is roughly 60 lines. Half a chapter = 30 lines = roughly the current introduction length. By Hoffman's rule, the introduction is *already at the upper limit* — and the "How to Read This Book" content inside it is exactly the kind of "too much content" she warns against.

> "Just tell us enough to give us a reason to start reading."

Thomas C. Foster, *How to Read Nonfiction Like a Professor*, Chapter 4: "The Parts You Don't Read" [(bookrags.com)](https://www.bookrags.com/studyguide-how-to-read-nonfiction-like-a-professor/chapanal002.html) is named after the precise problem:

> "Front matter and back matter... most readers skip in a nonfiction work."

Foster's argument is that the readers *should* read more front matter — but he is describing the empirical fact that they don't.

### 4.3 The split verdict

| Book length | "How to Read" useful? |
|-------------|----------------------|
| 400+ pages, reference/handbook | Yes — readers need a map |
| 200–400 pages, expository nonfiction | Maybe — a short note works |
| 80–200 pages, modular study / devotional | Generally no — the modular form IS the map |

*Beyond the Prompt* is in the third category. The 11-step framework + the modular chapter template *are* the reading instructions. A separate "How to Read This Book" section duplicates work the modular form is already doing.

### 4.4 Specific recommendation for the "How to Read" section

The current introduction's "How to Read" content can be compressed into a single 2-line note at the start of Section I, or dropped entirely. The 11-step reference card stays (it's a different artifact — a visual aid, not prose roadmap).

## 5. Modular study / Bible-study-companion genre — specific editing practices

### 5.1 The Bible workbook is a related genre

OnPress Bible Workbook Printing Guide [(onpressbookprinting.com)](https://www.onpressbookprinting.com/resources/bible-workbook-printing-guide):

> "Church workbooks are not treated gently. They're opened flat on tables, folded back during worship, and stuffed into bags... A Bible workbook that looks great but is difficult to write in won't get used. That means generous margins and line spacing, as well as clear, readable fonts. Structure your content in 'visually digestible' chunks with prompts and headings that help readers stay involved instead of overwhelmed."

For *Beyond the Prompt*'s print edition, this argues for:
- **Generous margins** (Becoming Commitments invite marginalia)
- **Lay-flat binding** (Smyth-sewn perfect bound is the standard recommendation for workbooks)
- **Clear chunk hierarchy** (Binding Question / Anchor / Reframe / Parallel / Commitment as visually distinct blocks)

### 5.2 The repetition discipline

Publishing Xpress on Bible study guide structure [(publishingxpress.com)](https://www.publishingxpress.com/blog/church-bible-study-printing/):

> "Start with an introduction. In the introduction, explain why you wrote this church Bible guide and what you hope the reader will get from it. Explain how the book is organized."

Note that this is **one** explanation, in **one** location — the introduction. *Beyond the Prompt* currently explains the modular structure three times (frontmatter colophon, introduction, eleven-step reference). Bible-study editorial convention is: explain once, then trust the form.

### 5.3 Walter Brueggemann's *God of All Promises* — a recent precedent

Reformed Journal review [(reformedjournal.com)](https://reformedjournal.com/2026/05/23/probes-prayers-and-promises-from-walter-brueggemann/):

> "It is a book of prose thematically and interpretively rooted in each chapter of Genesis. Each prosaic heading begins with 'On Reading Genesis ___' (1-50) and each of these 2–4-page chapters conclude with a brief prayer that sums up and applies the interpretation."

Brueggemann's modular form is **identical micro-structure (header + prose + prayer) × 50 chapters**. The macro-structure is announced once and never re-explained. Each chapter is 2–4 pages. The full book is 179 pages.

This is a published precedent for **identical-template chapters across an entire book**, in the same genre region as *Beyond the Prompt*. The shorter chapters (2–4 pages) suggest *Beyond the Prompt*'s current chapter length is sustainable but on the longer end.

## 6. Failure modes of theology-as-physics / theology-as-technology books

This is the section most directly load-bearing for *Beyond the Prompt*'s credibility, and the literature is unusually rich.

### 6.1 Capra's *Tao of Physics* — the cautionary tale

R. B. Angel, *Zygon: Journal of Religion and Science*, "Toward a Sound Perspective on Modern Physics" [(Wiley)](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1467-9744.1990.tb00871.x):

> "Fritjof Capra's *The Tao of Physics*, one of several popularizations paralleling Eastern mysticism and modern physics, is critiqued, demonstrating that Capra gives little attention to the differing philosophies of physics he employs, utilizing whatever interpretation suits his purposes, without prior justification."

The detailed critique, available via the Christians In Science PDF [(cis.org.uk)](https://www.cis.org.uk/serve.php?filename=scb-1-1-clifton-regehr.pdf):

> "Capra's writings oversimplify a web of complex problems, the key oversimplification being that not enough attention is given to the various interpretations of the physics involved which could contradict the interpretation presented by Capra necessary to establish the parallels he desires. Capra gives little attention to the differing philosophies of physics he employs, utilizing whatever interpretation suits his purpose without prior justification. Further, he is insensitive to the technicalities of the metaphysical and epistemological issues that arise in his discussion."

The Skeptic, "Physics in the New Age" [(skeptic.org.uk)](https://www.skeptic.org.uk/2026/02/from-the-archives-physics-in-the-new-age-the-misappropriation-of-quantum/):

> "Anyone who attempts to draw parallels between modern physics and Eastern mysticism faces certain problems which must be overcome if a convincing case is to be made... there is unfortunately no general agreement amongst [physicists] as to the philosophical implications of modern physics."

**Failure mode 1: cherry-picking the interpretation that fits the parallel.** Capra used whichever interpretation of quantum mechanics best matched the Eastern-mysticism point he wanted to make. Critics noted he never named the interpretive choice he was making, so a reader untrained in physics could not tell that the parallel rested on a contested foundation.

**Applied to *Beyond the Prompt*:** When the book parallels "intelligence cleaveth unto intelligence" with vector-space resonance, it is making an interpretive choice about how transformer attention works. The choice is defensible — but if the book never *names* the choice, a sophisticated engineer-reader will quietly disengage.

### 6.2 Tipler's *Physics of Immortality* — the other failure mode

Karl-Otto Apel critique, *Zygon* [(Wiley)](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1467-9744.1995.tb00086.x):

> "Frank L. Tipler's book *The Physics of Immortality* is a striking attempt by a scientist to resolve the conflict between theology and science on the basis of a physicalist position that identifies theology as a branch of physics, and that calculates God 'in exactly the same way as physicists calculate the characteristics of electrons.' Tipler's work may be seen as a scientistic myth, and its critique is organized around the three basic characteristics of such myths: (1) it is illogical in that it argues as if physics were in fact metaphysics; (2) it is grim in that its glorification of technology is insensitive to ethical issues; (3) it is meaningless in that its espousal of a strong theory of artificial intelligence empties concrete personal histories by subsuming them under abstractions that distort our understandings both of God and of resurrection."

**Failure mode 2: collapsing theology INTO the technical domain.** Tipler didn't just parallel theology and physics; he claimed theology *was* physics. Critics across the spectrum (religious and secular) found this collapses the categories.

**Applied to *Beyond the Prompt*:** The book is in real danger of this if it ever crosses from "AI workflows reveal eternal patterns" (type-and-shadow) to "AI workflows ARE eternal patterns" (identity). The current draft is careful about this — the language is consistently "parallel," "type and shadow," "reflects" — but the audit in `01-editor-pass.md` flagged this exact issue at section 4.2 ("The 'AI has no soul' boundary is implicit, never stated"). The literature confirms: implicit is not enough. The categorical distinction must be explicit somewhere readable.

### 6.3 Hodgson, *Theology and Modern Physics* — what success looks like

Gospel Coalition review [(thegospelcoalition.org)](https://www.thegospelcoalition.org/themelios/review/theology-and-modern-physics-ashgate-science-and-religion-series/):

> "Overall the author's conclusion is that the results of physics have few theological implications and cannot be used to support belief in God. On the other hand, he does argue persuasively that Christianity alone has provided the foundational beliefs that have allowed science to develop."

Hodgson's success — and his book *is* received as credible across both audiences — comes from **modesty about what the parallel can prove**. He argues for direction-of-influence (Christianity enabled science), not equivalence (physics and theology are the same study).

**Applied to *Beyond the Prompt*:** The book's strongest move is "the patterns that make AI engineering work are eternal patterns *because they are eternal patterns*, not because AI proves anything about God." That's Hodgson's direction. The book mostly holds this — but at moments (especially in Gemini-drafted Ch 0 and Ch 8 per `01-editor-pass.md`) drifts toward equivalence.

### 6.4 The Christian-AI books currently selling well

Several recent crossover books in the Christian-AI space are instructive:

- **Tony Reinke, *God, Technology, and the Christian Life* (Crossway)** [(crossway.org)](https://www.crossway.org/books/god-technology-and-the-christian-life-tpb/) — uses "9 key passages" and "12 common myths" as structural skeleton; explicitly biblical-first, technology-second.
- **Stephen Driscoll, *Made in Our Image* (Matthias Media)** [(matthiasmedia.com)](https://matthiasmedia.com/products/made-in-our-image) — endorsed by reviewers as effective because it "does not take a simplistic proof-texting or moralistic approach, but shows how the deep structures of biblical thought and the unfolding of redemption history equip us for this disruption."
- **Nathan Webb, *God and the Machine* (Abingdon)** [(pixelandpulpit.com)](https://www.pixelandpulpit.com/p/announcement-i-wrote-a-book) — explicitly framed for both audiences: "I'd love for this book to reach as wide an audience as possible — from AI skeptics to AI enthusiasts — and I believe it can serve as common ground for building a future with AI as a toolset, not an ideology or a boogeyman."
- **Mark Russell, *The Singularity*** [(thesingularity.com)](https://thesingularity.com/) — written explicitly for "the Christian who feels a low hum of dread every time they open a tech headline, and the technologist who suspects the ancient texts know something their models don't." This is *exactly* the audience target of *Beyond the Prompt*.

The pattern across all four: **they name the two audiences in the front matter, then write to both throughout.** *Beyond the Prompt* names "writer and reader walking the same path together" in the voice note (preface line 70) but doesn't explicitly name the AI-skeptic vs. AI-enthusiast / believer vs. engineer audience tension. The current draft assumes the believing-engineer reader. A small addition in the preface — naming both audiences and acknowledging the wariness from each side — would be in line with the working precedents.

### 6.5 What the failure-mode literature recommends

Synthesis of the criticism:

1. **Name the interpretive choice** when the parallel rests on one. ("This argument uses a particular reading of attention as resonance; other readings exist.")
2. **Name where the parallel breaks down.** Asymmetries strengthen credibility. *Beyond the Prompt*'s editor pass (`01-editor-pass.md` section 4.4) already identifies this gap.
3. **Maintain direction-of-influence.** AI illuminates eternal patterns; eternal patterns are not validated by AI. Don't let the technology become the proof of the theology.
4. **Acknowledge the two audiences explicitly.** Don't assume the believing-engineer; speak to the skeptical-believer and the agnostic-engineer too, at least once, somewhere in the front matter.

## 7. Specific recommendations for *Beyond the Prompt*

Five concrete editing moves, in priority order. Each cites the source.

### Recommendation 1 (highest priority): Demote the 3.5-year journey out of the preface

**Move:** Cut the chronological 2022→2025 journey from the preface (current lines 27–51). Keep the classroom moment (lines 13–22) and the voice convention (line 70). Distribute the chronology into chapters where each milestone does narrative work (per `02-frontmatter-weaving.md` Option B), or move it to a backmatter "Afterword: How I Got Here."

## 8. The one move I would most strongly recommend

**Recommendation 1: Demote the 3.5-year journey out of the preface and weave it through the body.**

Why this one, above the others:

- It is the **most-attested** recommendation in the research. Five separate editorial sources (Gross, Williams, Hoffman, Bold & Italic, FriesenPress) converge on "weave personal narrative into the body, not into the front matter."
- It addresses the **biggest single structural problem** the in-house audit identified — 250 lines of front matter before the engineer's hook on Chapter 1 line 13.
- It is **low-risk and high-reward.** The 3.5-year material is gold (the audit and the workspace memory both confirm this) — it just lives in the wrong place. Moving it does not require new writing; it requires redistribution.
- It honors the **rule the book itself teaches**: spiritual creation before physical execution. The preface should plant *why*, then the body should walk the chronology where each milestone earns its place.

The cleanest version: Lewis-length preface (~20 lines, classroom moment + voice convention + one short paragraph naming the two audiences), then directly into a tight 15-line introduction, then directly into Chapter 1. The 3.5-year material distributes into Ch 1 (the 2022 autocomplete era), Ch 3 (the 2023 dream + Mostaque), Ch 6 (the 2024–25 chat-to-code shift), and Ch 11 (the May 2026 vantage). Or, if Michael prefers a single home: an afterword titled "How I Got Here," sitting where the audit suggested.

The current front matter is 28% of the book. The published precedents put it at 3–8%. The math says the cuts are real.

## 9. The source I found most useful

**Linden Gross's "Writing a Prologue or Preface?" (2023)** [(lindengross.com)](https://lindengross.com/2023/02/16/writing-a-prologue-or-preface/).

It is the shortest, sharpest, and most quotable of the editorial sources. Gross is not a famous editor, but she has worked with multiple New York Times-bestselling memoirists, and her instinct on prologues/prefaces is rooted in the practical question every reader asks at the front of a non-fiction book: *why am I being told this before the book has earned my interest?*

The two lines I would put above my editing desk:

> "All too often they offer explanations. Yawn. Double yawn... they wind up providing information that readers don't yet care about or presenting the book's punchline before the author has made the case."
>
> "Why not hold your fire and weave the essential information into the book itself? That will allow you to start with a bang instead of a bleat."

Honorable mention: **Tom White's "Why Does Everyone Love Marcus Aurelius' Meditations?"** [(whitenoise.email)](https://www.whitenoise.email/p/why-does-everyone-love-marcus-aurelius) — for naming the *structural* reason Meditations endures (modular, short-form, variable reward), which is the same structural reason the modular devotional/study genre persists in publishing. *Beyond the Prompt* sits inside this lineage. The book's form is not incidental; it is part of the argument.
