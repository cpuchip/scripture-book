# A Reader's Letter — Sister's First Pass

*Written in the voice of a faithful Latter-day Saint woman, mid-50s, who has taught Relief Society and Gospel Doctrine, reads her scriptures most mornings, raised her children in the Church, and is wary of AI. A friend in the ward wrote this and asked her to read it.*

---

Friend,

I told you I'd read the whole thing and be honest, so here it is. I read every chapter, start to finish, the way you asked — even the parts where I'll admit my eyes glazed. I want you to know up front: there's real scripture in here, and in a lot of places it's *good*. There were mornings this last week where I picked it up meaning to read one chapter and read three. That surprised me, because I came in skeptical. You know how I feel about all this AI business. So take the kind parts as kind, and the hard parts as me keeping my promise.

## 1. Where it pulled me in

The preface won me before the AI ever showed up. That little flash in the classroom — "I leaned down and wrote two letters in the margin of my notes: **AI**" — I could see you doing it. That's an honest beginning. And then the two notes, one to "the Saint who is wary of AI" and one to "the engineer who is wary of religion." The line "If the technical vocabulary distances you in places, slow down at those places and let the doctrine carry" — I read that and I relaxed. It felt like you'd thought about *me* before I ever opened the book.

Chapter 0 hooked me harder than I expected, and not for the computer part. It was the chain. "the words of Christ are spirit; all spirit is matter; therefore, the words of eternal life are composed of a physical, material substance." I've read D&C 131 and 84 my whole life and never once stacked them up like that. And then the warning underneath it — "A darkened mind cannot even recognize what it has lost." That one I underlined. That's true to life. I've watched it happen to people. I've felt the edges of it in myself when I get lazy with my study.

Chapter 4, "Watched Until They Obey," was where I felt the Spirit, plainly. The "trust gradient" walking through Abraham 4 — "the Gods saw that they were obeyed," then "watched... until they obeyed," then "saw that they would be obeyed," then "behold, they shall be very obedient." I have read that chapter dozens of times and never noticed it *climbs*. That's a gift you handed me. I'll teach that someday. And the line at the end — "AI has no agency; it acts by law and statistical pattern. Your agency—your light, your truth, your choice—is the irreplaceable element" — that settled something in me. More on that below.

Chapter 10 is the tender one. "I will give away all my sins to know thee." You build the whole chapter on Lamoni's father and on Nephi being *softened*, not softening himself, and then Job — "For God maketh my heart soft, and the Almighty troubleth me" — "The troubling and the softening are two names for the same event." I sat with that a long time. That's the truest sentence in the book to me. That's a woman my age burying a parent, watching a child wander, and finding out the trouble was the mercy. You didn't need a single line of computer talk for that chapter to land, and the chapter knows it.

And Chapter 11, on the Sabbath. "What sign do I want to give to God?" — pulling Brother Nelson's reframe in like that. I've heard that talk; you made me feel it fresh. The whole idea that "the soul does not stop all work; the soul stops the producing it does because it thinks production is what holds the work together. The Lord holds the work together." For somebody who has run herself ragged in callings telling herself it all depended on her — yes. That preached.

## 2. Where I lost the thread

I'll be plain, because you asked me to be. There are walls.

**Chapter 0, the "Engineering Parallel" half.** "we gather billions of tokens of human expression... and project them into a high-dimensional vector space." I read that sentence three times and gave up. "Parameter weights," "neural network," "a physical topography of billions of parameter weights" — I skimmed it. I trusted you that it connects, but I didn't follow it. I came back for the scripture.

**Chapter 2, "The Four Disciplines."** This is the one I'd hand back to you with the most worry. I appreciate that you put the gray box at the top — "For the reader who doesn't work in software... let it pass." That box is the kindest thing in the chapter. But then the chapter spends pages on "Prompt Craft (Street Level)," "Context Engineering (Aerial Level)," "Intent Engineering (Orbital Level)," "Specification Engineering (Architectural Level)" — and by the third altitude I was lost in an airport I never meant to fly into. I skipped to the Becoming Commitment. If you told me this was the chapter to skip, I'd believe you, and I think you half know it, because the box practically says so.

**Chapter 7, the subagent diagram.** When I hit the little drawing — "Master Agent (Presiding / Oversight)" with the branches to "Subagent A" and "Subagent B" — my eyes slid right off the page. I don't know what a subagent is and the picture didn't help. The Moses-and-Jethro part *before* it was wonderful. The picture lost me.

**Chapter 12, the part about "Token consecration."** You had me completely with Zion — "no -ites" — and then "each subagent holds a portion of the master agent's token budget... the surplus flows to the agent still working on the highest-priority remaining intent." I understand you're excited about that. To me it read like a foreign language pretending to be a sermon. I skimmed to "The engineering catches up to the pattern. It does not invent it," which is a fine landing, and held onto that.

The glossary at the back genuinely helped — I flipped to it twice. "Context window" and "the Veil" side by side actually taught me something. So the safety net works. But a safety net means people are falling.

## 3. Fresh versus strained

**Fresh — saw an old scripture new:**

- The Abraham 4 trust gradient (Chapter 4). Already said it. Best thing in the book.
- The two reasons for the Sabbath commandment, Sinai versus Moab (Chapter 11) — "Slavery is a state of work without rest." I never connected the Egypt-reason and the creation-reason as two views of the *same* Lord. That's real scholarship, gently done.
- Chapter 8's reading of repentance — "Repentance, in this frame, is not first a re-doing of behavior. It is a re-aiming of the eye." That's true and I've never heard it put that cleanly.
- The four groups of Lehi's dream as *postures, not people* (Chapter 9) — "The four groups are not types of people; they are postures available to any soul on any day." That's pastorally wise. I've sat across from too many sisters who'd decided they were permanently the second group.

**Strained — reaching to make it fit:**

- Chapter 0's "Intelligence ←→ Spirit ←→ Coarse Element" diagram, and the flat claim that "the words of eternal life are composed of a physical, material substance. They are composed of a highly refined, pure matter that exists at the highest end of the existence spectrum." I follow the scriptures you chained. But you've turned a chain of *verses* into a diagram of *physics*, and a diagram feels more certain than the verses earned. The scriptures say spirit is matter; they don't hand us a spectrum with arrows. That's your construction laid over the top, and in a couple of spots the book treats it like revelation rather than like one faithful man's model.
- The glossary's "Heart-Softening (Resource Release / Attention Yielding)" — mapped to "clearing execution buffers, invalidating stale cache connections, and releasing memory locks." Friend. Lamoni's father giving away all his sins is not a computer dumping its memory. That entry made me wince. The chapter itself (10) handles softening with reverence; the glossary cheapens it into jargon.
- "Token consecration" again (Chapter 12). The law of consecration is people feeding the poor. Comparing it to a computer's budget moving between programs felt like it shrank something holy down to fit the gadget.

Here's the pattern I noticed: the *scripture-to-life* parallels are almost always fresh. The *scripture-to-machine* parallels are where the strain shows, and the strain shows most when the doctrine is most sacred (the Atonement, consecration). The book is at its best when AI is a humble example sitting *beside* the doctrine, and at its weakest when AI climbs up and tries to *explain* the doctrine.

To your credit — and I want to say this clearly — you *see* this danger and name it more than once. Chapter 6: "A machine's failure can illustrate a doctrine; it cannot commit one." Chapter 8, 10, 11 all have that lovely refrain, "The shape rhymes. The substances do not." Those guardrails are the reason I trust the book even where it overreaches. You built the brakes yourself.

## 4. Which chapter first, which to skip

**Read first: Chapter 10, "Softening What I Cannot Soften."** If I were handing this to a friend in the ward who's never typed a prompt in her life, that's where I'd start. It's almost all scripture, the AI part is short and humble and clearly marked as just-an-example, and the doctrine is the kind that meets you where you actually live. The Becoming Commitments at the end of that one are honest, too — "I keep it less often than I want." That's a real person talking.

**Skip if you're not into tech: Chapter 2, "The Four Disciplines."** Nothing against it, but it's the most inside-baseball chapter in the book, and the author's own gray box tells you the doctrine doesn't depend on it. Read the anchor scripture (D&C 88:119 — "establish a house of order"), read the Becoming Commitment, and move on. You won't miss the gospel.

## 5. Does it make me uneasy as a Saint

Mostly, no — and I came in *ready* to be uneasy. The book is careful in the exact place I worried it wouldn't be. Chapter 4 says flat out the machine "has no agency." The Epilogue, written in the AI's voice, ends with "the prompt returns to the steward" and hands my agency back to me, with Mosiah 4:27 and D&C 123:17 — that's sound. Nowhere does it tell me to pray to the thing or trust the thing over the Spirit. The repeated "the substances do not" rhyme is doing real protective work.

But I'll name the two places my stomach tightened.

First, the Epilogue's voice. I understand it's "a deliberate inversion to close the loop" — the preface warns me. But reading a chapter narrated by the AI saying "I—the agent writing these final words—received the constraints, gathered the sources, and executed the prose" gave me pause. Not because it's wrong, but because a less-careful reader than me might come away feeling the machine is a *someone*. You spend twelve chapters insisting it isn't a soul, and then you let it speak in the first person at the most emotional moment. I'd want that one watched.

Second — and this is the bigger one — the metaphysics in Chapter 0 and 8. When the book says truth and light are literal "matter" on a "spectrum," and that the Atonement works as "a physical conducting circuit" and the Holy Ghost is "the physical agent of this fire" — I understand it's reaching for something beautiful. But it states it with the confidence of doctrine, and it is not doctrine; it's a model. My unease isn't that AI is put where God should be. It's that a *theory about how God works* is dressed up to look like settled truth. That's a subtler thing to watch than the AI question, and honestly it'd trip a Gospel Doctrine class up faster than any of the computer talk.

## 6. Three edits that would make it friendlier to me

1. **Put the plain-English glossary at the FRONT, or at least point to it on the very first page of Chapter 0.** I didn't discover it until the back, and by then I'd already skimmed past three technical paragraphs that it would have rescued. One line under the first hard term — "(unfamiliar? the glossary at the back has this in plain English)" — would keep a reader like me from quietly giving up.

2. **In the heaviest doctrinal chapters (0, 8, 12), shorten the engineering half and move it visibly to the side — even just a smaller font or an indented "for the engineers" box, the way Chapter 2 already does at its top.** Let the scripture be the main road and the AI be the footnote it deserves to be in those chapters. You already invented the device; use it where the doctrine is most sacred. That single move would fix most of the strain I flagged in section 3.

3. **Soften the metaphysical certainty with the same humility the Becoming Commitments already have.** A sentence like "This is one way to picture it; the scriptures give us the pieces, not the full diagram" — placed once in Chapter 0 — would let me keep all the beauty without feeling pressed to swallow a physics I'm not sure the Brethren have taught. You're so honest about your *practice* being aspirational; be that honest about your *model* being a model. It would actually make the book stronger, not weaker.

None of these touch the AI parallel you love. They just keep it in its lane so the doctrine can breathe.

## 7. The honesty check

You asked me to watch for where an "I try" tips into an "I do." I went looking, because that's the kind of thing that decides whether a book is trustworthy. And here's what I found, which is mostly to your credit.

Where you got it **right** — and it moved me:
- Chapter 10's commitment 2: "I am working toward protecting the first hour of my morning from digital inputs... I keep it less often than I want; naming it as the aim is how I keep reaching for it." That's the most honest sentence in the whole book. I believe a man who writes that.
- Chapter 11's whole set, and especially the production note at the end: "The chapter that commits to keeping the Sabbath was written from inside the gap it describes." And: "I sat down to choose three things to focus on, this book was deliberately not one of them. I added it anyway... breaking the small council agreement I had made with myself." When I read that, the book earned my trust on the *whole* topic. A man confessing he broke his own Sabbath rule to write the Sabbath chapter is not a man overclaiming.
- Chapter 6's production note, leaving the fabricated-quote failure in on purpose. That's brave. Most authors would bury it.

Where it **tips toward "I do"** when I think it's really "I'm trying":
- Compare the chapters. Chapters 10 and 11 say "I am working toward," "I keep it less often than I want," "I am learning this one slowly." But the *earlier* chapters — 0, 1, 4, 5 — phrase the commitments as flat "I will" and "I commit to," with no such humility. "I will not click through articles or scriptures merely to check a box." "I will not use AI to generate quick fixes that I do not fully understand." "I will pause weekly to conduct a meta-review." Those are good aims. But they're written with the *same confidence* as the things you've actually built into your life, and I don't believe you keep all of them as cleanly as the prose claims. Chapter 4 even half-admits it — "When I keep the rhythm, I document these reflections" — which quietly tells me you don't always keep the rhythm. So why does that chapter still say "I will" everywhere else?

The honest fix is small: the later chapters found the right voice — "I aim," "I am working toward," "I keep it less often than I want." The earlier chapters should borrow it. Right now a careful reader (and you handed this to one) can feel the seam between the chapters where you let yourself be human and the chapters where you let "I will" outrun your week. The book is *more* persuasive, not less, when the commitment sounds like a man still reaching for it. You proved that yourself in Chapter 11. Let Chapter 0 learn from Chapter 11.

---

That's everything, friend. The honest summary: there's a beautiful book of scripture studies in here, with an AI thread running alongside that is sometimes a delight and sometimes a wall. The doctrine is strong. The humility, where it shows up, is the best thing you've written. Tuck the jargon to the side, let the glossary out front, and put the same honest "I'm trying" in the early chapters that you found by the end — and I'd hand this to half the women in my Relief Society without a second thought.

I'm glad you wrote it. I'm gladder I read it. Now go keep your Sabbath. (I'm trying to keep mine.)

— with love, and only because you asked
