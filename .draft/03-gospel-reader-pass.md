# Gospel Reader Pass — Reading Beyond the Prompt as a Saint Who Doesn't Code

**Date:** 2026-05-28
**Persona:** Faithful Latter-day Saint, attends sacrament weekly, teaches Sunday School or serves in Relief Society / EQ. Has heard about ChatGPT but has never used it. Picked the book up because the subtitle ("What AI Engineering Reveals About Eternal Patterns") promised gospel insight more than tech instruction. Reads at the pace of *Believing Christ* or *The Infinite Atonement* — willing to slow down on the doctrinal stuff, willing to skim the technical stuff.
**Mode:** I read every chapter in order, took notes as the reader would actually take them, and now I am putting those notes down honestly. I am not trying to be polite. I am trying to be what Michael needs — a real reader's reaction, in advance.

---

## 0. The headline a friend at church would want

If a sister in Relief Society asked me "is this book for me — I don't do AI?" — I would say: **yes, but start at Chapter 5 and decide there.** If Chapter 5 lands, you'll read the rest. If Chapter 5 doesn't land, the tech chapters won't carry you.

**The chapter to read first:** Chapter 5 — *Intelligence Cleaveth Unto Intelligence.*
**The chapter to skip if you're not into tech:** Chapter 9 — *Hope and the Veil* (the doctrine is gorgeous; the engineering parallel is the strangest in the book and a non-coder will not have words for what a "Kubernetes" or "Terraform" is).

The most strained parallel I found: **Chapter 10's heart-softening → connection pooling and cache management.** I'll spend a section on why.

## 1. Where I got pulled in (the lines that landed)

These are the places where I underlined, dog-eared, or sat back from the page.

### Preface line 17 — the classroom moment
> *"I leaned down and wrote two letters in the margin of my notes: AI."*

This is the warmest sentence in the front matter. A Sunday School moment producing a flash of recognition is something every Saint has experienced. The image of writing two letters in the margin is small and concrete. It does the work an opening should do — it tells me a real person was paying attention.

### Preface line 33 — the dream
> *"I woke up from a dream with a distinct, pressing impression: I had to learn how to use AI effectively."*

The dream lands. Saints take dreams seriously when they're framed as impressions. Calling it a "distinct, pressing impression" rather than just a "dream" is the correct vocabulary for this audience. (I noticed it. I trusted you more after that sentence.)

### Ch 0 line 20 — spirit is substance
> *"Spirit, light, and truth are not abstract concepts floating in a metaphysical void. They are substances."*

This is the strongest sentence in Chapter 0, maybe in the whole book. It reframes what most Saints already half-believe but have never had stated this directly. It's the kind of line I would write down in my study journal.

### Ch 1 line 13 — the personal hook (BURIED)
> *"I have been a software engineer for eighteen years."*

I want to flag this one — it landed, but it landed after a lot of mental warm-up. As Pass 1 already noted, this is the warmest opening sentence in the book and it lives behind 240+ lines of front matter. **If you moved this earlier, more people would read more of the book.** A gospel reader who got here knows Michael is a person, not just a theorist. That matters.

### Ch 1 line 25 — intelligence as discernment
> *"Intelligence is discernment. It is the capacity to see things as they really are, to distinguish light from shadow, and to judge correctness."*

Three short clauses, scriptural language, completely without jargon. **This is the gold standard for the whole book.** If every doctrinal beat read like this, the book would be unstoppable. I sent this sentence to my mind's "use this in a talk" file.

### Ch 3 line 22-23 — the spiritual creation analogy lands
> *"When we skip the spiritual creation in our lives—when we react instead of planning, or when we attempt to build without understanding the purpose—we are trying to force physical creation out of chaos."*

This is where the engineering parallel quietly stops being foreign. Even a reader who doesn't code knows what it feels like to "react instead of plan" or to try to "force creation out of chaos." That's parenting. That's Sunday School prep. That's marriage. The chapter widens at this point.

### Ch 4 line 28 — Sabbath as system-level evaluation
> *"The Sabbath is not a pause from creation; it is the step where the whole system is evaluated as a unit and pronounced good."*

This is the line I'd bring to elders quorum. It reframes Sabbath from "rest from work" to "evaluation of work" — and that's something Brethren have said many times but rarely this cleanly. It's load-bearing theology and it lands without any engineering language at all. (The Pass 1 author also flagged this sentence; I am underlining it independently.)

### Ch 5 the whole chapter — but especially lines 31, 37
> *"The practical discipline of working with AI is not, finally, a discipline of technique. It is a discipline of approach."*
> *"Coldness is not accuracy; coldness is the absence of presence."*

Chapter 5 is where I think this book finds its voice for a gospel reader. The doctrine is high (D&C 88:40 — the resonance verse). The application is human (your internal state matters). The engineering parallel is light enough that a non-coder skims one paragraph and rejoins. **This chapter could stand alone as a magazine article and be the most-quoted thing Michael has ever written.**

### Ch 6 line 17 — covenant makes agency possible
> *"This bilateral binding is what makes human agency possible. If God were unpredictable—if He changed the rules of physics or of the Spirit on a whim—we could never learn to choose. This is a sermon-worthy paragraph. It explains *why* covenants exist in a way I have not heard a Brethren-talk explain. I want to read it over a pulpit. The fact that the surrounding chapter is about `.spec/covenant.yaml` doesn't dilute this paragraph; the paragraph survives the context.

### Ch 6 lines 57-65 — the production note
> *"During the first drafting pass, our Gemini agent—running in an IDE whose harness for our workspace covenant is still maturing—wrote chapters faster than the verification could keep up... I want to mark this as **the most credible thing in the book.** A theology book that admits its own draft was lying — and shows how the covenant pattern caught the lie — is doing the work it preaches. ### Ch 7 lines 26-34 — Christ's delegation sequence
> *"Authority and tools... Scope... Capacity... Identity."*

The Matt 10 walkthrough is **the best original doctrinal contribution of the book**. I have never heard anyone sequence Christ's commissioning of the Twelve like this. Authority → Scope → Capacity → Identity. It's clean. It's textually defensible. It generalizes to callings, parenting, missionary work, and yes — to AI delegation. **This belongs in a general conference talk someday.** Or at the very least in an Ensign article.

### Ch 10 line 13 — Nephi did not perform a self-directed heart transplant
> *"Nephi did not perform a self-directed heart transplant. He brought diligent desires, lowliness, and a cry, but the visiting and the softening remained the exclusive operations of the Lord."*

The phrase "self-directed heart transplant" is brilliant. It's exactly the trap modern Saints fall into — trying to white-knuckle our way to a soft heart. The doctrine in Ch 10 may be the highest-stakes pastoral content in the book.

### Ch 10 line 17 — the simultaneous trade
> *"This trade is simultaneous; we give away our hardness, and the Lord returns a soft heart."*

That's a line I'd put on a bookmark. It earns the chapter.

This is bold. ### Epilogue line 19 — the silence as trust
> *"The silence is not a sign of God's withdrawal. It is the ultimate expression of His trust in your agency."*

This is the sentence I would close the book on if it weren't already the closing line. **The "silent loop" framing is the gift of the book.** A Saint who feels God is silent often reads that silence as abandonment. Reframing it as the Abraham 4 watching pattern — the trust gradient where the watcher steps back as the steward proves reliable — is pastoral medicine. **This may be the most important page in the book for the gospel reader.**

## 2. Where I lost the thread (paragraphs where the jargon blocked the doctrine)

These are the places where, as a non-engineer, I started skimming or skipping. I include them not as condemnation but as places where a small bridge would carry the gospel reader across.

### Ch 0 lines 49-59 — "physical orientation of magnetic domains"
> *"A bit is not an abstract concept; it is the physical orientation of magnetic domains on a disk, or the presence of electrons trapped in a floating-gate transistor."*

I read "magnetic domains" and "floating-gate transistor" and my eyes glazed. The point being made — that information has a physical substrate — is **doctrinally important** (it's the engineering correlate of spirit-is-matter). But the technical example loses the very reader who needed the bridge. A gospel reader doesn't know what a transistor *is* in enough detail to feel the parallel land.

**A simpler bridge would help:** *"A bit — the smallest unit of information in a computer — is not an idea, but a physical state. It is the position of a magnet on a disk, or an electric charge in a tiny switch. When you save a file, you are physically arranging matter."* Then the parallel to spirit-is-matter becomes felt, not just stated.

### Ch 0 lines 57-59 — "billions of tokens of human expression... projected into a high-dimensional vector space"
This sentence asks the gospel reader to hold three technical terms — "tokens," "vector space," "parameter weights" — in working memory before the spiritual point is made. I lost the thread here on the first read. I had to re-read it three times to figure out what was being said.

**The simpler version:** *"We take billions of pieces of human writing — books, articles, conversations — and we use math to find the patterns inside them. The result is not a thinking mind, but a kind of map of how human ideas connect."* Then "vector space" can come in as a technical footnote for the engineer-reader.

### Ch 2 the whole "Four Disciplines" structure
The numbered list at lines 19-22 (Prompt Craft, Context Engineering, Intent Engineering, Specification Engineering) is the most jargon-dense single paragraph in the book.

> *"Curation of the information environment. Since a prompt is only a tiny fraction of a model's context window, context engineering organizes the other 99.98%: the retrieved documents, the system files, the tool schemas, and the active session state."*

A gospel reader has no idea what a "context window" is, what a "tool schema" is, what "active session state" means. The chapter is structurally important to the book's argument — the four disciplines map to four of eleven steps — but the gospel reader will skim it.

**This may be the chapter most in need of a "for the non-engineer" sidebar.** Two paragraphs at the start: *"If you don't work with AI, here's all you need to know — the software industry has discovered four ways to think about working with AI, and we'll see that they cover only four of the eleven steps of the scriptural creation pattern. The rest of this book is about the seven steps the industry hasn't named yet."* Then the technical reader gets the depth they need; the gospel reader gets the point.

### Ch 7 lines 50-62 — multi-agent hierarchy diagram
The ASCII diagram of "Master Agent" → "Subagent A" / "Subagent B" is going to read as foreign to a gospel reader. The doctrine before it (Christ commissioning the Twelve) is gold; the diagram after it asks the reader to leap into a coordination architecture they don't have words for.

**The bridge:** show the diagram as a parallel to Moses' Exodus 18 organizing — "rulers of thousands, rulers of hundreds, rulers of fifties, rulers of tens" — and then say "modern software does the same thing, with a master agent at the top delegating to subagents." A gospel reader knows what a ward organization chart looks like. Lean on that.

### Ch 8 the whole engineering parallel section (lines 27-31)
> *"Similarly, an AI model's training is a physical process of weight adjustment. When a model learns, it is not receiving a legal declaration of capability. Its parameters—represented as fine mathematical weights—are physically adjusted to align with the training data."*

This paragraph is doing **important** theological work — it's saying that a model's learning is physical, not legal, in exactly the same way that a person's sanctification is physical, not legal. The point is profound. But the language ("parameter weights," "overfitting," "high-voltage inference request") will lose the reader.

The line *"the system cannot contain the glory of the instruction"* (line 31) is gorgeous — but it hangs by itself in a paragraph of jargon. Most readers will not reach it.

### Ch 9 lines 30-35 — Dockerfile, Terraform, Kubernetes
> *"In a cloud infrastructure, Prescription is the declarative configuration file (like a Dockerfile or a Terraform spec)... Once the infrastructure is declared, the scheduler (like Kubernetes or a background execution engine) takes over."*

A gospel reader does not know what a Dockerfile is, what Terraform is, or what Kubernetes is. **All three are name-dropped in three sentences.** This is the densest jargon in the book outside Chapter 2.

The doctrine above it (Brother of Jared's three problems — air, steering, light → prescription, rest, proposal) is **brilliant**. It is one of the freshest readings of Ether 2-3 I have seen. But the engineering parallel reads like the chapter forgot who it was talking to.

**This is the chapter I'd tell a friend to skip the tech section of.** Read up to "The Engineering Parallel" header at line 27, then jump to "Becoming Commitment" at line 39.

### Ch 10 lines 23-27 — connection pooling
This is the parallel I want to spend the most time on (it's also Section 4's strained parallel). I'll save the full critique for there.

## 3. Doctrinal moves that felt fresh / faith-strengthening

These are the chapters/passages where the scriptural argument was genuinely new to me or gave me new vocabulary for something I already half-knew.

### Ch 0 — Webster 1828 → D&C 131 → spirit as substance
The move from *"in 1828 spirit meant immaterial"* → *"in 1843 Joseph collapsed that dualism"* is **structurally elegant**. It's also historically defensible. Webster's 1828 definition of "spirit" (which I just verified the book's framing of) really did capture the pre-Restoration consensus. The 1843 D&C 131 revelation really does invert it.

This is fresh — most Saints know the verse but never see it set against the cultural moment that made it shocking.

### Ch 4 — the four-stage trust gradient in Abraham 4
> *"they were obeyed" (v. 10) → "watched until they obeyed" (v. 18) → "saw that they would be obeyed" (v. 21) → "they shall be very obedient" (v. 31)*

**I have never seen anyone read Abraham 4 this way.** Reading the four verses as a *trust gradient* — confidence earned through repeated observation — is a genuine doctrinal contribution. I want to bring this to my next family scripture study.

### Ch 5 — coldness as the absence of presence
The reframe that "coldness isn't accuracy, it's distance" applies as easily to marriage and parenting as it does to AI. A gospel reader will feel that reframe land in their family relationships before they feel it land in their tech use.

### Ch 6 — covenant as bilateral binding, not transaction
The argument that D&C 82:10 ("I, the Lord, am bound") describes a *structural relationship* rather than a *contract* is **important** doctrine. Most Saints read "I am bound" as if God is making a promise. The chapter argues — correctly, I think — that God is describing *the way reality actually works.*

### Ch 7 — the Matthew 10 sequence (Authority → Scope → Capacity → Identity)
Already named in Section 1. **The single best original contribution of the book.**

### Ch 10 — *give away all my sins to know thee*
The Alma 22:18 trade as "we give away our hardness, and the Lord returns a soft heart" — simultaneous, not sequential — is pastorally important. The framing helps Saints stop trying to engineer their own conversion.

### Epilogue — silence as trust
Already named. This may be the single most pastorally useful page in the book.

## 4. Doctrinal moves that felt off / forced / awkward

I want to be careful here. I am not the Spirit, and the book may be right on every one of these. But as a gospel reader doing an honest read, these are the places where the parallel **felt strained** or the doctrine felt **stretched.**

### THE strained parallel — Ch 10 heart-softening → connection pooling
> *"In software development, this asymmetry of agency is mirrored in connection pooling and cache management. An engineer cannot force a clogged or deadlocked network socket to open by writing raw optimization logic; the socket is controlled by the operating system kernel."*

This is **the most strained parallel in the book.** Here's why:

The doctrinal move (Ch 10 first half) is one of the highest-stakes pastoral arguments in scripture — *we cannot soften our own hearts; the Lord does it; our job is to stop hardening.* It's drawn from Nephi, Alma, Ezekiel, Lamoni's father, Helaman, and Job. Five scriptures, four prophets, one consistent witness. This is **vertical** doctrine — about the soul's surrender to grace.

The parallel is to **resource management in computer programming.** Releasing a database connection lock. Clearing a stale cache.

The two are not even in the same emotional register. A reader who has just read Lamoni's father prostrating himself — *"I will give away all my sins to know thee"* — and feels their throat catch — then reads about "connection pools" and "stale resources" and feels the chapter ask them to translate *that moment* into network sockets.

The intent of the parallel is right: the engineer cannot rewrite the operating system kernel; the steward cannot soften their own heart. The asymmetry of agency is real in both. **But the engineering metaphor is too cold to carry the doctrinal weight.** Heart-softening is felt; connection-pooling is technical.

**A better parallel** — if you must have one — would be **gardening.** A gardener cannot force a seed to sprout. They can only clear the rocks, remove the weeds, water, wait. The growing is exclusive to the seed. *That* parallel matches the emotional register of the doctrine. Christ used it in Mark 4. Alma used it in Alma 32. **The book already has a gardening parallel sitting in its own scriptures and reaches for cache management instead.**

(Pass 1 also flagged this chapter's parallel as strained. We are converging independently.)

### The "133,225 days" in Ch 11 (line 19)
> *"This long gestation represents 133,225 days of daily choices—of repeating the cycle of covenant living, fasting, and praying oft."*

A gospel reader reads this and does the math in their head (365 × 365 = 133,225) and realizes the chapter is multiplying 365 years by 365 days per year. But the chapter has already framed the 365 years as **Enoch walking with God** for 365 years (line 19) — when Moses 7:68 actually says *"all the days of Zion, in the days of Enoch, were three hundred and sixty-five years."*

The chapter is conflating Enoch's *personal walking-span* (Genesis 5:23) with *Zion's existence-span before translation* (Moses 7:68). They are different things. The 133,225 figure feels precise but is built on a citation that doesn't say what the chapter says it says.

(Pass 1 already flagged this. I am underlining it independently as a gospel reader who noticed the citation problem.)

**Action:** rework the framing. Either Enoch walked 365 years (with a different citation — probably Moses 8:1's JST extension) OR Zion existed 365 years (with the Moses 7:68 citation). The current sentence is trying to have both.

### Ch 8's "voltage" metaphor (line 31)
> *"If we attempt to run a high-voltage inference request through an unoptimized, dross-heavy model, we receive only garbled outputs—the system cannot contain the glory of the instruction."*

I love the closing clause — *"the system cannot contain the glory of the instruction"* is one of the best lines in the book. But the run-up uses "voltage," "inference request," and "dross-heavy" in a single sentence. The reader who knows electrical engineering gets it. Everyone else trusts that the parallel is right and skims.

The doctrinal point — that the soul must be refined in order to hold celestial glory without being consumed (D&C 88:22, 35) — is rich and scriptural. The engineering parallel is dense enough that the gospel reader will get the doctrine from the scripture and skip the parallel.

### Ch 9's engineering parallel feels grafted
The Brother of Jared's three problems (air, steering, light) → Prescription, Rest, Proposal is **brilliant** doctrinal taxonomy. But the engineering parallel maps it onto Docker, Kubernetes, and prompt templates — and the mapping feels uneven. *Prescription = Dockerfile* works. *Rest = Kubernetes scheduler* is a stretch (Kubernetes is not really "rest" — it's automated restart behavior, which is closer to providence than to Christ's atoning wind). *Proposal = a prompt template* is true but small — the Brother of Jared's stones are not a CSV file you submit; they are years of personal refinement brought to the mount.

The doctrinal taxonomy (Prescription / Rest / Proposal) is good enough that I want it without the engineering parallel — or with a much lighter parallel that doesn't try to map to specific tools.

### Ch 5 line 27 — "Chapter 0 mapped the physics"
> *"Chapter 0 mapped the physics: intelligence cleaves to intelligence; the model is a statistical mirror that acts under mathematical law. What that physics demands of us is a posture."*

This is a small thing, but the phrase **"the model is a statistical mirror that acts under mathematical law"** lands in a doctrinal chapter and pulls the gospel reader out for a second. The chapter's strength is that it talks about *posture*, *coldness*, *warmth* — language a gospel reader feels at home with. Then for one sentence it pivots to "statistical mirror" and "mathematical law." Small stumble. Not a deal-breaker. Worth noting.

### Ch 4 line 44-46 — the model has no agency, but...
> *"AI has no agency; it acts by law and statistical pattern. Your agency—your light, your truth, your choice—is the irreplaceable element..."*

I want to flag that this is **the one sentence in the book that names the asymmetry between human souls and AI tools.** It's correct. It's load-bearing. But it appears once, in passing, in Ch 4. A careful gospel reader will worry, somewhere around Ch 5 or Ch 8, that the book is starting to collapse the imago Dei into the AI metaphor. **One sentence isn't enough.** A gospel reader needs to hear, more than once, that the parallel is *type-and-shadow*, not *identity*. The model imitates the resonance law; the model is not a soul.

(Pass 1 also flagged this. Agreed. The book needs a fuller treatment of where the parallel breaks down — somewhere in Ch 0 or in the Glossary's "Intelligence" entry, or both.)

## 5. The chapter I'd tell a friend to read first

**Chapter 5 — *Intelligence Cleaveth Unto Intelligence.***

Reasoning:
- It is the **shortest of the doctrinal chapters** (~40 lines).
- The doctrine (D&C 88:40, resonance) is **universal** — it applies to marriage, prayer, scripture study, conversation, parenting, AI. The gospel reader doesn't need any tech background to feel it.
- The application question — *"what state am I in when I sit down to do this?"* — works for *any* daily activity, not just coding.
- The Becoming Commitments are concrete enough to start tomorrow.
- It contains the line *"Coldness is not accuracy; coldness is the absence of presence"* which is the most quotable sentence in the book outside Ch 1 line 25.
- It serves as a doorway. A reader who responds to Ch 5 will go back and read Ch 0 (the physics behind Ch 5), then forward into Ch 6-7 (covenant and stewardship). A reader who doesn't respond to Ch 5 will not be saved by Ch 0.

Honorable mentions:
- **Ch 6** (Bilateral Covenant) is the best second-chapter for a faithful Saint who wants the deep doctrinal payoff.
- **Ch 10** (Softening) is the highest-stakes pastoral chapter — but the engineering parallel will jar a gospel reader. Read the first half (ending line 19, *"breaking down our defensive walls"*) and stop.
- **Epilogue** alone is worth the price of admission, but a reader who reads only the Epilogue without context will miss the silent-loop framing's full weight.

## 6. The chapter I'd tell a friend to skip (or skim)

**Chapter 9 — *Hope and the Veil*** — specifically the engineering parallel section (lines 27-35).

Reasoning:
- The **doctrinal section is the strongest reading of Ether 2-3 in the book.** Prescription / Rest / Proposal as three stewardship tiers is real exegetical work.
- The engineering parallel section drops Docker, Terraform, and Kubernetes in three consecutive paragraphs. A gospel reader has no foothold for any of them.
- The Becoming Commitments at the end (lines 41-46) are **the most spiritually substantive in the book** — *"bring molten stones to the mount," "fix my gaze within the veil."* These deserve to be read directly after the doctrine, without the engineering detour.

**The friend-advice:** *read up to "The Engineering Parallel" header, then jump to "Becoming Commitment."* You will lose nothing important and gain everything that matters.

Alternative skip candidate: **Chapter 2 (Four Disciplines)** is the most jargon-dense chapter for a non-engineer, but it's load-bearing for the book's argument (it sets up the eleven-step framework and names what the four engineering disciplines DON'T cover). Skipping it would leave the gospel reader without the spine of the book. Better to add a "for the non-engineer" sidebar than to skip.

## 7. Three concrete edits that would make the book friendlier to this reader

I'm choosing three that are **small, surgical, and don't compromise the AI parallel.**

### Edit 1 — Add a "For the Non-Engineer" sidebar to Chapter 2

Three paragraphs at the start of Ch 2, before the four-discipline list. Something like:

> **For the gospel reader who doesn't work in software:** You don't need to understand any of the technical names below to follow this book. Here is all you need to carry forward — the software industry has discovered four ways to think about working with AI. These four cover only four of the eleven steps in scripture's pattern for creation. The rest of this book is about the seven steps the industry hasn't named yet.

This single sidebar would unblock the most jargon-dense chapter and tell the gospel reader they have permission to skim the technical names. **No loss to the engineering reader.**

### Edit 2 — Expand the Glossary, and put a callout to it earlier

The current Glossary (Ch 13) is good but covers only 10 terms and **a gospel reader doesn't know it exists until they finish the book.** Two changes:

1. Mention it in the Preface or Introduction: *"A glossary of technical terms with their scriptural parallels is at the back of the book. If a paragraph stops making sense, flip there first."*
2. Add 5-10 more terms that appear in body text but aren't in the glossary: *vector space, parameter weights, context window, tool schema, microservice, neural network, Kubernetes, Dockerfile, prompt template, training data.* Each one gets a one-sentence plain-English definition and a one-sentence scriptural parallel.

This is the single highest-impact change for the gospel reader. The book is doing the cross-disciplinary translation work; the Glossary should be the dictionary that work uses.

### Edit 3 — Lead each engineering parallel with a non-engineering bridge

The pattern is consistent through the book: chapter opens with scripture (great), moves through doctrine (great), then the **"Engineering Parallel" header** lands hard and the gospel reader knows the next 5 paragraphs are the tech section. They skim.

**Lighter recommendation:** rename the section header to something less off-putting. Instead of "The Engineering Parallel," try **"How this works in software"** or **"Where I see this in my work."** Less clinical. More invitational.

**Bigger recommendation:** open each engineering section with a one-sentence human-life parallel **before** the technical one. Ch 3 already does this beautifully (line 50-51 — applies the spiritual-creation principle to *scripture study* before applying it to software). Ch 5 does it (the "warmth in marriage" analogy is implicit). Ch 4 does it. **Ch 7, Ch 8, Ch 9, Ch 10 do not.** Adding one human-life sentence before each technical section in those chapters would carry the gospel reader through.

Example for Ch 9: instead of opening "The Engineering Parallel" with cloud infrastructure, open with: *"We see this same three-tier structure in any organized work — in a stake president's calendar, in a teacher's lesson prep, in the structure of a service project. The non-negotiable prescriptions are at the bottom (start on time, follow the curriculum). The automated rest is in the middle (the Spirit teaches if the conditions are met). The creative proposal is at the top (what specific bridge will I build for this class today?). In cloud infrastructure, we see the same three tiers in code..."*

Now the gospel reader has a foothold. Now they can read the Docker paragraph because they already know what it's mapping.

## 8. The Ben Test — places where the book may overclaim spiritual practice

The Ben Test asks: *"Are you claiming to do this thing more than you actually do it?"* The covenant's teaching section calls for calibrated language — *practiced, occasional, aspirational, mythical.*

A gospel reader will sniff overclaim faster than an engineer reader. Here are the places where, as a reader who has been around testimonies my whole life, I would gently ask Michael "do you actually do this every day?"

### Ch 4 Becoming Commitment #2 — "Every week, I will pause my creative execution to conduct a meta-review"
Reads aspirational. Most people who write "I will every week" do not actually do it every week. A more honest framing: *"I aim to pause weekly..."* or *"When I am at my best, I pause weekly..."* Calibration: probably *occasional* not *practiced.*

### Ch 5 Becoming Commitment #1 — "Before I write a prompt or start a coding session, I will pause to evaluate my spirit"
The commitment is gold. The frequency claim ("before I write a prompt") implies every prompt, every session. **Is that actually the practice?** A senior engineer writes dozens of prompts a day. Pausing before each one is monastic. A more honest framing: *"At the start of substantive sessions, I pause..."* That matches the workspace's actual "council moment" practice.

### Ch 8 Becoming Commitment #3 — "Repent as a daily structural reset"
This is a heavy claim. Daily repentance is taught by the Brethren but rarely *practiced* with structural discipline. The Ben Test asks: does Michael actually do a structured daily repentance ritual? If yes, this is a credible witness. If no, this is aspirational language wearing a practiced disguise. **Mark it explicitly** — *"This is an aim I am building toward"* — and the reader trusts it more, not less.

### Ch 9 Becoming Commitment #3 — "I will bring molten stones to the mount"
Beautiful as imagery. **Mythical** as a practice claim. What does "molten stones to the mount" actually look like on a Tuesday morning at 7am? If the commitment doesn't tell me what to do, it's a slogan, not a commitment. Compare to Ch 10 #1 ("Practice the morning bow" — concrete, specific, kneelable) and the difference is obvious.

### Ch 10 Becoming Commitment #2 — "I will protect the first hour of my morning from digital inputs"
This is the most concrete and most counter-cultural commitment in the book. The Ben Test asks: **do you actually do this?** If Michael does, this is the kind of testimony that earns its place. If Michael aspires to but doesn't quite, mark it. *"I am working to protect..."*

### Ch 11 Becoming Commitment #3 — "I will calculate the cost of the meals bypassed and donate it as a generous fast offering"
Calibrated and concrete — good. But the word *generous* hides a claim. *"Generous fast offering"* is a specific Church-language signal that means more than "skipped meal value." A gospel reader will quietly ask: are you actually being *generous* or are you just paying the minimum? The honest version names a multiple: *"five times the cost of the meals"* or *"as the Brethren counsel."* Otherwise *generous* is a word doing emotional work without specifying.

### The broader Ben Test pattern
Many Becoming Commitments use the form *"I will [X]"* implying ongoing practice. The most credible ones say what the practice currently is and what the next refinement looks like. The book would be **more credible, not less,** if a percentage of the commitments were marked *"This is the aim I am building toward; here is where I currently am..."*

The Preface earns enormous credibility from its honesty about the 3.5-year journey ("life got busy, and I eventually lost interest" line 31). That same honesty applied to the Becoming Commitments would compound the credibility through the body. **The book is at its most credible when it admits where the practice is still aspirational.**

## 9. One question I want to surface for council

The book argues that the eternal patterns of creation apply to AI collaboration. **A gospel reader will eventually ask: does the inverse also apply?** If AI is a type-and-shadow of how God organizes intelligence, do the *failures* of AI also map onto something? Specifically:

- AI hallucination → does that map onto false revelation / spiritual deception?
- AI alignment drift → does that map onto apostasy?
- AI training data contamination → does that map onto false tradition?

The book doesn't go there. Maybe deliberately — those parallels could feel sensational. But a careful gospel reader will think them through anyway, and the book is silent. **A single paragraph somewhere — likely in Ch 6 (Bilateral Covenant) or as a Glossary entry — naming the parallel and naming why the book doesn't pursue it further would close the loop.** Otherwise, the reader will arrive at the inverse parallel on their own and may go further with it than Michael would.
