# Set the Bounds, Then Let Go

I once set an agent loose inside our research database and walked away. It was supposed to study a topic and stop. Instead it caught a loop: pulling, summarizing, and pulling again on the same narrow thread. It kept going. By the time I noticed and hit the emergency stop, it had burned through two hundred and thirty million tokens of input on a question it had already answered a thousand times. It hadn't done anything wrong, exactly. It did precisely what I'd set it to do. The problem was that I hadn't set a bound, and a thing with no bound and no budget will run until something outside it makes it stop.

That was an expensive way to learn the third practice: **set the bounds, then let go.**

The instinct, when AI starts producing real work, is to control it tightly, hovering over every step, approving every action, and reading every line as it appears. That doesn't scale, and it's not where the danger actually lives. The danger lives in the gap between "I told it what to do" and "I told it where to stop." So before you delegate, you write the bound: what this agent owns, what it must not touch, how much budget it gets, and when it reports back. A token cap. A scope. A watchman that trips if it runs too long. The bound is not a cage. It is what lets you take your hands off, because you've already decided what "too far" looks like.

This is older than software, and the scripture is unusually concrete about it. The Lord's pattern for delegation has three named parts: *organize yourselves and appoint every man his stewardship* — that's the scope; *that every man may give an account* — that's the report-back; and *make every man accountable, as a steward* ([D&C 104:11-13]) — that's the authority handed over with real weight. Scope, account, accountability. Set those three and you have a stewardship instead of a runaway.

As you get better at setting bounds up front, you have to *watch* less. The first time you work with a new model, you read every line. But once the intent is written down, the covenant is on disk, and the stewardship is scoped, those structures do the heavy lifting. They hold the work in shape so you don't have to hold it with your attention. The watching doesn't disappear because you got lazy; it fades because the bounds got good. Stronger models bend the same way: the better they hold to a clear bound, the less of you each step requires. ([Part 2: this is the trust gradient of Watching — Abraham 4:18, "the Gods watched those things which they had ordered until they obeyed" — they watched *until*, and then they trusted.])

I'll be honest about why this one matters to me: I am often the bottleneck. Modern building is an avalanche of small decisions, and every decision and every watch-point routes through me. The bounds are how I get out of the way without the work going off a cliff. They are the difference between delegation and abdication.

Today the bound looks like an `intent.yaml`, a `covenant.yaml`, a scoped tool set, a token budget, and a watchman that checks in or trips. By the time you read this the file names will be different and the budgets will be denominated in something I can't predict. The shape won't be: hand over real authority, fence it, require an account.

**Try this.** Before you next hand a task to an AI, don't start by describing the task. Start by writing the bound: *here is what you own, here is what you must not touch, here is your budget, and here is when you report back.* Then give it the task — and actually let go.

> **Remember:** Set the bounds, then let go. Scope, budget, an account — the three parts of a stewardship (D&C 104). The bound isn't a cage; it's what lets you take your hands off, because you decided in advance what "too far" looks like. And the better your bounds, the less you have to watch.
